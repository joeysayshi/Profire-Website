Smoke alarms are the one piece of fire safety equipment almost every apartment resident sees every day, yet they cause a surprising amount of confusion in strata buildings. Is the owners corporation responsible, or the lot owner? What changes when the apartment is rented out? This guide walks through the **smoke alarm rules for NSW strata apartments**, so strata managers, owners and landlords know what's required and who should be doing what.

## What the law requires

In NSW, smoke alarms must be installed in every building where people sleep. That covers owner-occupied homes, rental properties and apartments. The required locations and specifications are set out in Part 13 of the *Environmental Planning and Assessment (Development Certification and Fire Safety) Regulation 2021*.

The basics, as explained by Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW):

- **At least one smoke alarm on every level** of the home, even a level without bedrooms.
- **Alarms in hallways near bedrooms.** If bedrooms are in different parts of the home, each area needs an alarm.
- **No hallway?** Then the alarm goes between the bedrooms and the rest of the home.
- **Every alarm must meet Australian Standard AS 3786**, the standard for smoke alarms.

These are minimums. FRNSW recommends going further, with interconnected alarms in every bedroom and living area (including hallways and stairways), so that when one alarm detects smoke, they all sound.

For most apartments, which sit on a single level, that means at least one compliant alarm in the hallway or area outside the bedrooms. Two-storey apartments and townhouse-style units need at least one on each level.

## Smoke alarm rules for strata: who is responsible?

The regulation places the duty to install smoke alarms on the building owner. In a strata scheme, how that plays out depends on the building and on how the alarms were installed, which is why the answer isn't the same everywhere.

A few things shape it:

- **How the alarms are powered.** Some apartments have battery-only alarms. Others have alarms hard-wired to the building's mains power, which can only be repaired or replaced by a licensed electrician.
- **Whether the alarms are part of the building's fire safety measures.** Smoke alarms can be listed as a fire safety measure on a building's fire safety schedule. If they are, they need to be maintained and are covered by the building's annual fire safety statement (AFSS), which the owners corporation arranges each year.
- **What your scheme has agreed.** Owners corporations differ in how they handle in-unit alarms. Some take on repair and replacement across the building; in others, it's left to each lot owner.

If you're a strata manager or committee member and you're not sure where your building stands, start with the fire safety schedule and the scheme's records. They'll tell you whether smoke alarms are listed as a fire safety measure and how they've been handled in the past.

## Extra rules when an apartment is rented

Landlords in NSW have specific smoke alarm obligations under the residential tenancy laws, and they've applied since 23 March 2020. According to NSW Fair Trading guidance, a landlord must:

- Make sure smoke alarms (including heat alarms) are **in working order**. This responsibility can't be handed to the tenant in the tenancy agreement.
- Have at least one alarm in a hallway outside a bedroom, or another suitable location, on each storey. Alarms **cannot be removed or disabled**.
- **Repair or replace** a faulty alarm, including a flat removable battery, within **two business days** of finding out it isn't working.
- **Check alarms every year.**
- **Replace removable batteries every year**, or for lithium batteries, at the interval the manufacturer specifies.
- **Replace alarms within 10 years** of manufacture, or sooner if the manufacturer says so.
- Use a **licensed electrician** for hard-wired alarms.

Landlords must give tenants at least two business days' notice to inspect or assess an alarm, and at least one hour's notice to repair or replace one.

### The strata exception

In a strata scheme, those landlord rules apply unless **all four** of these conditions are met:

1. The alarms are hard-wired, or battery-operated with the owners corporation responsible for repairs and replacements.
2. The landlord has told the tenant in writing that the owners corporation is responsible for repairs and replacements.
3. The landlord notifies the owners corporation within 24 hours of becoming aware that a repair or replacement is needed.
4. The landlord takes reasonable steps to make sure the repair or replacement is carried out.

Tenancy agreements that started on or after 23 March 2020 must say whether the owners corporation is responsible for repairing and replacing smoke alarms. That's why it's worth the owners corporation being clear with lot owners about who handles in-unit alarms: landlords rely on that answer when they sign up tenants.

## Keeping smoke alarms working

FRNSW's maintenance advice is simple and applies to every apartment, rented or not:

- **Every month:** press and hold the test button for a few seconds to check the battery and the alarm work.
- **Every six months:** vacuum the alarm to remove dust. This helps prevent false alarms and keeps smoke able to reach the sensor.
- **Every year:** replace removable batteries. Alarms with built-in lithium batteries don't need this, as the battery can't be replaced.
- **Every 10 years:** replace the whole alarm, or sooner if the manufacturer specifies a shorter life. FRNSW explains that the sensitivity of all smoke alarms reduces over time.

FRNSW also warns that a common mistake is putting alarms too close to kitchens or bathrooms, where cooking fumes and steam can set them off. If an apartment's alarm keeps going off when someone cooks, the answer is to fix the location or type of alarm, not to disable it.

Old alarms can be dropped off for free at a Household Chemical CleanOut event or a Community Recycling Centre.

## A quick checklist for strata managers

1. **Check the fire safety schedule.** Find out whether smoke alarms are listed as a fire safety measure for your building.
2. **Confirm who handles in-unit alarms.** Make sure lot owners, and the landlords among them, know whether the owners corporation is responsible for repairs and replacements.
3. **Know what's installed.** Battery or hard-wired? Interconnected or standalone? How old are they?
4. **Plan for replacement.** If many alarms were installed at the same time, they'll reach the end of their life at the same time.
5. **Keep records.** Testing dates, faults and replacements make it much easier to answer questions from owners, tenants and your AFSS practitioner.
