Burnt toast, a steamy shower or a tradesperson cutting tiles near a detector can all send a fire truck to your building. If the building's fire alarm is monitored and there's no fire, the owners corporation or building owner may receive a bill. This guide explains how **false fire alarm charges in NSW** work, who ends up paying, and the practical steps that stop unwanted alarms before they happen.

## Why Fire and Rescue NSW charges for false alarms

Many apartment blocks, offices and commercial buildings have an automatic fire alarm (AFA) system that is monitored. When a detector activates, the signal goes through an approved Automatic Fire Alarm Service Provider (AFASP) to Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW), and a crew is sent. FRNSW has a statutory duty to attend every fire alarm.

The scale is large. FRNSW says it responded to about 58,000 automatic fire alarm activations in 2025, and roughly 98% of them were unwanted alarms, meaning crews arrived and found no fire or other emergency. These call-outs make up about 40% of all FRNSW responses.

FRNSW's point is not only cost. Unwanted alarms tie up crews who could be needed at a real emergency, disrupt residents and businesses, and can make occupants complacent, so they're slower to leave when an alarm is genuine.

## How false fire alarm charges work

At the time of writing, FRNSW's website lists the false alarm charge in NSW as **$1,656**. It applies to call-outs to monitored AFA systems, and not every unwanted alarm is chargeable. Alarms caused by events outside the owner's control, such as storms and other natural disasters, are not charged.

Two leniency rules apply:

- **60-day leniency:** the first false alarm in a 60-day period is not charged. Further false alarms within 60 days of that first one are charged.
- **24-hour leniency:** repeat false alarms within 24 hours are treated as one event, so only the first is charged. Only one leniency applies in any 24-hour period.

FRNSW reviewed its fees for attending false alarm call-outs in 2025 as part of a new Fire and Rescue NSW Regulation, so check FRNSW's website or ask your monitoring provider for the current figure before relying on it.

## Who pays in a strata building?

FRNSW doesn't bill the building directly. It invoices the AFASP, and the AFASP invoices the building owner or manager. The party that holds the contract with the AFASP is responsible for the charge. In a strata building, that is usually the owners corporation.

FRNSW says it does not support passing charges on to individual occupants, although it notes exceptions may apply. If your building has a recurring problem in one apartment, the better long-term fix is to find out why the detector keeps activating there, not just who to send the bill to.

## What causes unwanted alarms?

FRNSW lists these as the common causes:

- Poor ventilation, cooking fumes, burnt toast and steam
- Aerosol sprays, candles, incense, smoking and vaping near detectors
- Tradespeople and cleaners working near detectors, and dust
- Dirty smoke detectors
- Damaged break glass alarms or manual call points
- System malfunction, poor maintenance, or not enough maintenance in harsh environments
- Insect infestation

Several of these are about how people use the building. Others point straight back to the condition of the system.

## How to reduce false alarms in your building

FRNSW's guidance for owners, managers and occupants comes down to a few habits:

1. **Keep the system maintained.** FRNSW names poorly maintained systems as a primary cause of false alarms. Have a qualified technician test and maintain the system to the relevant Australian Standards. In NSW, AS 1851-2012 has been mandatory for routine servicing of fire protection systems in class 1b and class 2 to 9 buildings since 13 February 2026.
2. **Manage work on site.** Ask contractors to register and be inducted, and make sure any isolation of the alarm system during dusty, hot or steamy work is done correctly and reinstated afterwards. FRNSW recommends an on-site works management plan covering welders, blowers and similar equipment.
3. **Tell residents and staff.** People should know the building has an automatic fire alarm and where the detectors are. Simple reminders help: open fans, vents or windows before cooking or showering, never leave cooking or a toaster unattended, and keep aerosols and smoking away from detectors.
4. **Keep a log.** Record the date, time, location and cause of every unwanted alarm. Patterns show up quickly, such as one detector next to a kitchen or a fault that returns after rain.
5. **Fix the cause.** If the same detector keeps activating, the answer may be relocating it, changing the detector type, or repairing faulty equipment. Have that work done by a qualified person.

If your building is having a lot of unwanted alarms, FRNSW's alarms team can give advice on (02) 9742 7550 or alarms@fire.nsw.gov.au.

## Can a false alarm charge be waived?

Yes, in some cases. FRNSW can waive all or part of a charge at the Commissioner's discretion. The firefighters who attend can't waive charges on the spot; the request goes through your AFASP.

FRNSW's waiver guideline sets out the grounds. They include a leniency that should have applied, an event beyond your control, and rectification work such as replacing or relocating a detector, repairing equipment, or changing practices at the premises. For repair and replacement work, you'll need a tax invoice showing the date, cost, location and description of the work. The guideline also says that evidence of routine maintenance on its own isn't grounds for a waiver, and some waivers are capped at 75% of the charge.

Timing matters. Applications must be made within 180 calendar days of the FRNSW invoice date, so don't let a bill sit in the inbox.
