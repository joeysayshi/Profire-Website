When the power fails during a fire, exit signs and emergency lighting are what help people find their way out of a dark, smoky building. They're easy to forget because they sit quietly on the ceiling for years. This guide explains **exit signs and emergency lighting testing** for NSW buildings: which standards apply, what a test involves, how it ties into your annual fire safety statement, and what strata managers and building owners can do to stay on top of it.

## Why exit signs and emergency lighting matter

Emergency lighting comes on automatically when normal lighting fails, so people can see well enough to reach an exit. Illuminated exit signs show the way to that exit. Both run from a backup power source, usually batteries in each fitting, and both only work in an emergency if those batteries and lamps are in good condition.

The National Construction Code (NCC) sets out where they're needed in new buildings. Under Part E4 of NCC 2022 Volume One, emergency lighting is required in places such as:

- every fire-isolated stairway, passageway or ramp
- every required non-fire-isolated stairway
- corridors in Class 2, 3 and 4 buildings (such as apartment buildings) where the distance from a unit's door to an exit is more than 6 metres
- larger storeys in Class 5 to 9 buildings, such as offices, shops and warehouses.

Exit signs must be clearly visible to people approaching an exit, and be installed on, above or next to the doors that lead out of the building or into required exits.

The NCC says required emergency lighting and illuminated exit signs must comply with **AS/NZS 2293.1**, the Australian and New Zealand Standard for system design, installation and operation. The exact requirements for your building depend on when it was built and approved.

## Which standard covers testing?

Testing and maintenance are a separate question from installation. Two documents matter here.

**Your building's fire safety schedule.** In NSW, the fire safety schedule lists every fire safety measure in the building and the standard of performance it must meet. Building Commission NSW says the standard for each measure is set out in the schedule, and that measures need to be maintained to the standard that applied when the building was built. Exit signs and emergency lighting usually appear on the schedule as separate measures.

**AS/NZS 2293.2.** Part 2 of the standard sets the performance criteria for the routine service and maintenance of emergency lighting and exit sign systems. The current edition, AS/NZS 2293.2:2019, also introduced the concept of routine service, including preventative maintenance, and procedures for intervals greater than 12 months.

One point that often surprises people: **emergency lighting isn't covered by AS 1851**, the standard used for most routine fire safety maintenance. Building Commission NSW gives emergency exit lighting as an example of a measure not covered by AS 1851-2012. For those measures, owners still have to maintain them to the standard in the fire safety schedule or the original design.

## What does emergency lighting testing involve?

The exact routine depends on your system and the standard on your schedule, but testing generally covers two things.

### A visual check

The technician walks the building and checks that every exit sign is lit, clean, legible and not blocked by signage, fit-outs, stored goods or decorations. Emergency light fittings are checked for damage, missing diffusers and indicator lights showing a fault.

### A battery discharge test

The normal power supply to the fittings is cut so they switch to battery power. Each fitting then has to stay lit for its required duration. As an example, the City of Cockburn's emergency lighting guidelines, based on AS 2293.2, say fittings must stay lit for no less than two hours when batteries are new and no less than 90 minutes in later tests. A fitting that dims or goes out early fails, and usually needs a new battery or a replacement fitting.

Faulty fittings should be repaired and retested, and every test should be recorded. The same council guidelines require a logbook of inspection, service and maintenance to be kept on the premises and be available for inspection, and say all emergency lighting maintenance must be done by a suitably qualified and experienced person.

## How it ties into your annual fire safety statement

Every year, NSW building owners (Class 1b to Class 9 buildings) must issue an [annual fire safety statement](/afss-inspections/) covering each measure on the fire safety schedule, including exit signs and emergency lighting.

City of Parramatta explains that under section 81 of the Regulation, **the building owner is responsible for maintaining each fire safety measure** to its required minimum standard of performance. The statement confirms that each measure has been assessed by a practitioner holding the relevant accreditation, and a copy goes to council and the Commissioner of Fire and Rescue NSW.

In practice, that means regular testing during the year makes the AFSS much smoother. If your logbook shows tests have been done and faults fixed, the assessment is quicker, and there are fewer surprises when the statement is due.

## A simple checklist for strata managers and owners

- **Find your fire safety schedule** and check which standard applies to exit signs and emergency lighting.
- **Make sure testing is booked** at the intervals your standard requires, not just once a year before the AFSS.
- **Check the logbook** is on site, up to date and shows failed fittings being repaired.
- **Walk the common areas** now and then. A sign that isn't lit, or one hidden behind new signage, is easy to spot.
- **Tell your contractor about changes** such as new fit-outs, partitions or extra rooms, which can affect where signs and lights are needed.
- **Budget for batteries and fittings.** Batteries don't last forever, and older fittings may need replacing when they fail.
