Every year, strata buildings in NSW go through an **annual fire safety inspection** so the owner can issue an annual fire safety statement (AFSS). The inspection itself is the practitioner's job, but how smoothly it goes depends a lot on the preparation done beforehand. A little planning means fewer return visits, fewer surprises and less pressure on your due date.

Here's a practical guide for strata managers, building managers and owners corporations.

## What the annual inspection is for

An AFSS is a statement from the building owner confirming that each essential fire safety measure in the building has been assessed and is performing to the standard listed in the building's fire safety schedule. Councils describe it as being needed at least once every 12 months, and it must be lodged with council and with Fire and Rescue NSW. A copy, along with the current fire safety schedule, must also be prominently displayed in the building.

Two details shape your planning:

- **The assessment window.** The standard AFSS form says the assessment and inspection must have been carried out within the three months before the date the statement is issued. Inspect too early and the results can't be used; leave it too late and there's no time to fix defects before the due date.
- **Who does it.** The assessment must be carried out by an accredited practitioner (fire safety). In NSW this accreditation is provided through the Fire Protection Accreditation Scheme (FPAS) run by Fire Protection Association Australia.

Late statements are an offence, and councils issue penalty notices that increase week by week. So it pays to start early.

## Step 1: Check your due date and book early

Find the date of your last AFSS (it's on the copy displayed in the building, or your council can tell you). Count back three months from the next due date and book your inspection early in that window. That leaves time to arrange repairs if anything fails.

If your fire safety schedule lists **critical fire safety measures**, these may need a supplementary fire safety statement at shorter intervals. Make sure those dates are in your calendar too.

## Step 2: Gather the paperwork

Having the right documents ready saves time on the day. Pull together:

- **The current fire safety schedule.** It lists every measure the practitioner will assess and the standard each one must meet. If you can't find it, your council can usually provide a copy.
- **Last year's AFSS** and any supplementary statements.
- **Routine maintenance records**, such as logbooks and service reports from your regular [routine fire inspections](/routine-fire-inspections/). These show the measures have been maintained through the year, not just checked once.
- **Records of any work since the last inspection**, like repairs, upgrades or building alterations that may have affected fire safety measures.

## Step 3: Organise access to every area

The most common cause of delays is not being able to reach something. Before the inspection, make a list of every place the practitioner will need to get into, and sort out keys, swipe cards and contacts for each:

- Plant rooms, pump rooms and switch rooms
- The fire indicator panel and sprinkler valve rooms
- Roof access, basements and car parks
- Garbage rooms, storage cages and service risers
- Any locked common areas

If your fire safety schedule includes measures inside apartments, such as smoke detectors or self-closing fire doors on unit entries, the practitioner may need to enter individual units. Ask your inspection provider exactly what they need, then give residents plenty of notice with the date, a time window and what to expect.

## Step 4: Walk the building and fix the easy things

A quick walk-through a few weeks before the inspection can catch common issues while there's still time to fix them. Look for:

- **Blocked exits and corridors.** Bikes, furniture, boxes and rubbish in stairwells or paths of travel are a frequent problem. Exits and escape routes need to stay clear.
- **Fire doors that don't close properly.** Look for doors that are wedged open, closers that are broken or doors that don't latch.
- **Exit signs and emergency lights** that are dark, damaged or missing.
- **Fire extinguishers and hose reels** that are missing, obstructed or have been moved.
- **Damage to walls and ceilings** around service penetrations, especially after recent trade work.

You don't need to diagnose anything technical yourself. The goal is simply to clear obstacles and note anything obviously broken, so it can be raised with your provider before the visit.

## Step 5: Tell the people who need to know

Fire system testing can involve alarms, pumps and isolation of systems. Let residents, tenants and any on-site staff know when testing is happening. If your fire alarm system is monitored, check with your provider how the monitoring will be handled during testing so it doesn't trigger an unnecessary call-out.

Also confirm who will meet the technicians on the day and who they should contact if they find a problem.

## Step 6: Act quickly on defects

If the inspection finds a measure that isn't performing to the required standard, it will need to be fixed so the measure can be assessed as performing to the standard on your fire safety schedule. A clear defect report makes this much easier to manage, especially when you need to get quotes approved by a strata committee.

At Profire, our inspection reports are photographic, so you can see each defect for yourself. Our technicians can also carry out [fire equipment repairs](/fire-equipment-repairs/) so the work can be done and re-checked within your three-month window.

## Step 7: Lodge and display

Once the statement is issued, make sure it's:

1. **Lodged with your local council**, following the council's process.
2. **Lodged with Fire and Rescue NSW** using the online submission form on its website, together with the current fire safety schedule.
3. **Displayed prominently in the building**, along with the current fire safety schedule.

Keep a copy with your strata records and set a reminder for next year's inspection.
