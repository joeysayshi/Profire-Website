If you manage or own a building in NSW, there's one document that quietly drives your whole fire safety program: the **fire safety schedule**. It decides what gets tested, what standard each item is tested to, and what goes on your annual fire safety statement (AFSS). Yet when an inspection comes around, it's often the one piece of paper nobody can find.

Here's what a fire safety schedule is, what's on it, and where to look for yours.

## What is a fire safety schedule?

A fire safety schedule is the official list of the fire safety measures that apply to your building. Think sprinklers, hydrants, fire detection and alarm systems, exit signs, emergency lighting, fire doors and so on.

Under the Environmental Planning and Assessment (Development Certification and Fire Safety) Regulation 2021, a schedule must:

- **Cover the whole building**, not just the part affected by the latest approval.
- **List current and proposed measures**, and distinguish between measures already in place and those that are proposed or required.
- **Set the minimum standard of performance** for each measure, which is the standard it has to be designed, installed and maintained to.
- **Identify any critical fire safety measures**, along with how often they need to be assessed (at intervals of less than 12 months) through a supplementary fire safety statement.

In short, it's the rulebook for your building's fire safety. Everything else, including routine servicing and the AFSS, is measured against it.

## When is a fire safety schedule issued?

A schedule isn't something an owner writes. It's issued as part of an approval or order, and it's taken to form part of that document. NSW councils list the usual triggers as:

- A development consent for a change of building use (for example, a shop becoming an office)
- A complying development certificate for building work or a change of use
- A construction certificate for building work
- A fire safety order issued by council

Because buildings change over time, a building can end up with a newer schedule that replaces an older one. The current schedule is the one that counts.

## Why it matters for your AFSS

Every year, the owner of a building with fire safety measures must issue an annual fire safety statement to council and Fire and Rescue NSW. Before that happens, an accredited practitioner assesses each measure.

The schedule is where they start. The practitioner works out the required standard of performance for each measure from the fire safety schedule, and the measure must be assessed as performing to a standard no less than the one specified there. If the schedule is missing, out of date or wrong, the whole assessment is built on shaky ground.

## Where to find your fire safety schedule

If you don't have a copy to hand, try these places:

1. **On display in the building.** You must display your fire safety statement and the current fire safety schedule in a prominent place in the building. Check near the fire indicator panel, the main entry or the building manager's office.
2. **With your last AFSS.** The statement is issued together with the schedule, so last year's paperwork should include a copy.
3. **With the approval documents.** Look through the development consent, complying development certificate, construction certificate or fire safety order for the building. The schedule is attached to it.
4. **Your local council.** Many councils keep fire safety schedules on file and some publish them online. Wollongong City Council, for example, lets you search by address on its online portal and download the schedule from the attachments. Randwick City Council offers a similar search and a fire safety team you can call. If you can't find it online, contact your council's fire safety or building team.

## What if it's lost, wrong or doesn't exist?

**If it's lost or destroyed,** council can reissue it. Councils can also reissue a schedule to correct errors or omissions. A principal certifier can reissue one too, but only if an occupation certificate hasn't yet been issued. Councils generally ask you to apply on their form, and a fee may apply.

**If it doesn't match the building,** that's more common than you'd think. An accredited practitioner may find a measure that's installed but not listed, a listed measure that isn't installed, or a standard of performance that looks incorrect. Industry guidance from FPA Australia says the practitioner should tell the owner in writing, and the owner then needs to take steps to bring the schedule and the building into line.

Keep in mind that a reissue can't be used to fix errors that come from building work or from changes to the plans for the fire safety measures. Those usually need a proper review, so it's worth engaging a registered certifier to work out what's required.

**If your building has no schedule at all,** which can happen with older buildings, the owner can ask council for a schedule of the original fire safety measures for the building. In the meantime, any fire safety measure that applies to the building but isn't on a schedule must still be maintained to the standard it was originally designed and installed to.

## A quick checklist for owners and strata managers

- Find your current fire safety schedule and keep a digital copy with your building records.
- Make sure the schedule and your latest fire safety statement are displayed in the building.
- Check whether the schedule lists any critical fire safety measures, as these need supplementary statements more often than once a year.
- Read the schedule against what you can see on site. If something looks missing or extra, raise it before your AFSS is due, not the week before.
- Keep [routine fire inspections](/routine-fire-inspections/) up to date so every measure on the schedule is ready to be assessed.
