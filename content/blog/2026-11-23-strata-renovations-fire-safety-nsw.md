Renovations are a normal part of strata life. Kitchens get updated, floors get replaced and common areas get refreshed. But building work is also one of the easiest ways for a building's fire safety measures to be damaged, switched off or blocked without anyone noticing. This guide covers **strata renovations and fire safety**: what the approval rules say, where fire safety tends to go wrong during works, and what strata managers and building managers can do to keep the building protected while the trades are in.

## How NSW strata renovation rules work

The NSW Government's strata renovation rules sort work into three types.

- **Cosmetic work** doesn't need approval. Examples include painting the interior, laying carpet, filling minor holes and cracks in internal walls, and installing hooks, handrails, blinds or built-in wardrobes.
- **Minor renovations** need approval from the owners corporation (or the strata committee, if a by-law allows it). Examples include kitchen renovations, changes to internal walls, changes to recessed light fittings, new hard flooring, and wiring, cabling, power or access points.
- **Major renovations** can only be approved by the owners corporation, by special resolution. They include changes that affect the outside or structure, work that needs waterproofing, changes to the ceiling, and work that needs approval under other laws, such as council approval. Bathroom renovations and installing a false ceiling are both listed as examples.

When applying for a minor renovation, owners are asked to show plans of the work with dates and times, and the qualifications and details of the tradespeople. If major work changes the structure, the owner must give the owners corporation 14 days' written notice before it starts. The NSW Government also notes that any strata building work must be done safely and meet building standards.

The renovation rules don't mention fire safety directly. But look at that list again: internal walls, ceilings, light fittings, cabling. Those are exactly the places where fire-rated construction, penetrations and detectors live.

## Why fire safety still applies during works

In NSW, the building owner (in a strata scheme, the owners corporation) must keep each essential fire safety measure maintained to the standard in the building's fire safety schedule. That duty sits in section 81 of the *Environmental Planning and Assessment (Development Certification and Fire Safety) Regulation 2021*, and it doesn't pause because a contractor is on site. Since 13 February 2026, the measures must also be inspected and tested in line with AS 1851-2012.

Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW) has published a fire safety guideline on fire safety during construction work. It was issued in 2019 for public comment, and its scope includes "fire safety in existing occupied buildings undergoing construction work", including alteration and refurbishment. It notes that works on an existing building, "especially where parts of that building remain occupied, pose much greater risks". That describes most strata renovations.

## Where fire safety goes wrong during renovations

### Smoke detectors and false alarms

Dust, heat and steam from building work can set off smoke detectors. FRNSW lists tradespeople and dust among the common causes of unwanted alarms, along with cutting wires, spraying and steam cleaning. It says it responded to about 58,000 automatic fire alarm activations in 2025, and around 98% were unwanted. At the time of writing, its website lists the charge for a chargeable false alarm at **$1,656**. Read more in our guide to [false fire alarm charges in NSW](/blog/false-fire-alarm-charges-nsw/).

FRNSW's advice is that all workers on site "must register with the building owner or manager and be inducted into the workplace processes, ensuring appropriate isolation of AFA systems and responsible work-site activities." It also says to manage work that produces dust, heat or smoke so detectors aren't activated.

The flip side matters just as much. A detector that's been isolated or covered for the day is a detector that won't respond to a real fire. Any isolation should be limited to the area being worked on, and the system should be restored as soon as the dusty work stops.

### Penetrations in fire-rated walls and ceilings

New cabling, plumbing, downlights and air conditioning often mean new holes in walls and ceilings. In an apartment building, some of those walls and floors are fire-rated, and an unsealed penetration can let fire and smoke spread between units. Fire seals protecting openings in fire-resisting construction are an essential fire safety measure in their own right. Our post on [passive fire protection and penetrations](/blog/passive-fire-protection-penetrations-collars/) explains how they work.

### Fire doors and exits

Fire doors get propped open for deliveries, have their closers removed, or are damaged by trolleys and equipment. Corridors and stairs fill up with materials. FRNSW's guideline says "safe evacuation routes are to be provided and maintained always", and that while fire and smoke doors may be held open during works, "during fire these doors must be closed".

### Hydrants, sprinklers and other systems

Larger works sometimes need part of a system shut down. The FRNSW guideline says that when a hydrant system is isolated for building works, "valves must be reopened as soon as works are complete", and that a fire safety plan should identify "interim measures for when an existing system is being isolated".

### Hot work

Grinding, welding and other "heat or spark producing" processes, which FRNSW calls hot work, add a real ignition source to a building full of dust and packaging. Keep work areas tidy and confirm with the contractor how they'll manage hot work safely before it starts.

## A practical checklist for strata managers

1. **Ask about fire safety at approval.** When reviewing a minor or major renovation application, ask whether the work touches walls, ceilings, floors or services, and how fire-rated construction and detectors will be protected.
2. **Check the fire safety schedule.** It tells you which measures are in the affected area, such as detectors, fire doors, fire seals or sprinklers.
3. **Register and induct contractors.** Make sure trades know where the fire panel is, who to call before isolating anything, and that exits and fire doors must stay clear.
4. **Control isolations.** Agree in advance who isolates detectors, for how long, and who confirms they're back in service at the end of each day.
5. **Inspect when the work is finished.** Have the affected area checked for unsealed penetrations, damaged fire doors and covered or missing detectors before the next routine service or AFSS.
