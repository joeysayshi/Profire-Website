Fire safety equipment does its job best when the people in a building know what to do the moment an alarm sounds. That's what an emergency plan is for. If you manage a commercial building, run a business or look after a facility, this guide explains **emergency plans for NSW workplaces**: who needs one, what it has to cover, where AS 3745 comes in, and how to keep the plan working year after year.

## Does every workplace need an emergency plan?

Yes. SafeWork NSW is clear that it is a legal requirement for all workplaces in NSW to have an emergency plan. The duty comes from the work health and safety laws, specifically regulation 43 of the WHS Regulations, which Safe Work Australia's guidance on emergency plans is written to support.

FPA Australia's information bulletin on the topic gives the same answer: emergency plans are required in every state and territory except Victoria, and in any building where the approved Performance Solution calls for one.

The duty sits with the business or undertaking running the workplace. If you're a strata manager or building owner, your residential building might not be a "workplace" in the everyday sense, but any business operating in it, and any shared commercial areas, still needs to be covered.

## What a NSW emergency plan must cover

According to SafeWork NSW, an emergency plan must include:

- **Emergency procedures**
- **Regular testing** of those procedures
- **Information, training and instruction** for relevant workers

The emergency procedures themselves must cover:

- An effective response to an emergency
- Evacuation procedures
- Notifying emergency services at the earliest opportunity
- Medical treatment and assistance
- Effective communication between the person coordinating the response and everyone at the workplace

Safe Work Australia's guidance suggests a plan *may* also include contact details for wardens and first aid officers, how people will be alerted (such as a siren or bell), how you'll help people with hearing, vision or mobility impairments, and a map of the workplace showing fire protection equipment, emergency exits and assembly points.

That last item is where your building's fire safety measures meet your emergency plan. A plan that tells people to use an exit is only as good as that exit, its signage and its emergency lighting. Keeping those measures maintained through [routine fire inspections](/routine-fire-inspections/) is part of making the plan work in real life.

## Tailor the plan to your building

There's no one-size-fits-all emergency plan. Safe Work Australia says your plan should reflect:

- The nature of the work being done
- The hazards at the workplace
- The size and location of the workplace
- The number and make-up of workers and other people on site

It also suggests thinking about external hazards and any relevant state disaster plans. A small office, a warehouse and a busy medical centre will end up with very different plans.

## Where does AS 3745 fit in?

You'll often hear AS 3745-2010 *Planning for emergencies in facilities* mentioned alongside emergency plans. FPA Australia explains that the standard is not directly referenced by the model WHS Act or Regulations. However, the Code of Practice *Managing the Work Environment and Facilities* specifically directs businesses to refer to AS 3745.

According to FPA Australia, AS 3745 covers things like:

- A description of the facility
- Response procedures, including for natural disasters
- The roles of the Emergency Control Organisation (wardens and the like)
- The facility's emergency features
- Training and evacuation exercises

FPA Australia also notes that health care facilities have their own standard, AS 4083-2010, and that a building's Performance Solution may require an emergency plan prepared to AS 3745 or AS 4083. If your building has a Performance Solution, check its documentation so your plan meets any conditions it sets.

## Multi-tenanted buildings: coordinate with your neighbours

In a shared building, one tenant's plan isn't much use if the business next door is heading for a different exit. SafeWork NSW says businesses in shared workplaces should consult with each other, and suggests considering a **master emergency plan** for tenants, for example in a multi-tenanted office building.

For facility managers, that usually means:

- Agreeing on common assembly points and evacuation routes
- Making sure every tenant knows how the building's warning system sounds
- Sharing warden contact details across tenancies
- Running evacuation exercises that involve the whole building

## Train people and test the plan

A plan sitting in a folder won't help anyone in an emergency. SafeWork NSW says workers must be adequately trained, which can include practising evacuations and identifying assembly points. Training should reach:

- New workers, and existing workers through refresher training
- Shift and casual workers
- Short-term contractors and visitors
- Wardens and first aiders

The plan's procedures also need to be tested regularly. Evacuation drills are the simplest way to find out whether people can hear the alarm, know where to go and can account for everyone at the assembly point.

## Review and update the plan

Emergency plans go out of date. SafeWork NSW says to review and revise your plan regularly, and in particular when:

- The workplace changes, such as a fit-out or new layout
- The number of workers changes
- New work activities start
- After a real emergency

Safe Work Australia adds that you must maintain the plan so it stays effective, and that testing is another good trigger for a review.

## Keep your fire safety measures ready too

An emergency plan relies on the building's fire safety measures working when they're needed: alarms that sound, exit signs that light up, doors that close and hydrants that deliver water.
