If your building has fire hydrants or sprinklers, you've probably seen a technician open a valve and send water across the car park, or heard about a "5-yearly" test that needs extra time and planning. This guide explains **fire hydrant and sprinkler testing** in plain terms: what the main tests are, why some happen every year and others every five years, and what you're responsible for as an owner or strata manager in NSW.

## Why hydrants and sprinklers need regular testing

Hydrant and sprinkler systems spend almost all their lives sitting still. Pipes stay full (or empty, in a dry system), valves stay shut and pumps stay off. That's exactly why they need testing: problems like seized valves, blockages, leaks or a pump that won't deliver enough pressure usually stay hidden until someone checks.

In NSW, hydrants and sprinklers are typically listed as essential fire safety measures on a building's **fire safety schedule**. According to NSW Planning, the schedule sets out each measure in the building and the **minimum standard of performance** it must meet, and the building owner is responsible for maintaining each measure to that standard.

## AS 1851: the routine service standard

The Australian Standard for maintaining these systems is **AS 1851-2012, Routine service of fire protection systems and equipment**. It sets out service schedules for each type of system, including what to inspect and test and how often. Standards Australia released an amendment in November 2016, which the Victorian Building Authority describes as correcting editorial errors, clarifying the "baseline data" requirements and making minor clarifications to the service schedules.

For hydrants and sprinklers, AS 1851 has separate sections. Queensland's government test form for these systems, for example, records hydrant flow tests under Section 4 of AS 1851 and sprinkler tests under Section 2, and classes each maintenance test as either **annual** or **5-yearly**.

Your contractor works through these schedules on a regular [routine inspection](/routine-fire-inspections/) program, so the bigger annual and 5-yearly tests are part of a wider cycle rather than one-off events.

## Fire hydrant and sprinkler testing: the main tests

### Flow tests

A flow test checks that the system can actually deliver water at the flow and pressure it was designed for. Water is flowed from the system while the technician measures flow and pressure, and the results are compared with the system's design and its earlier results so that any decline in performance shows up.

If your building has a **dry hydrant system** (pipes that are normally empty and only charged when the fire brigade connects), Fire and Rescue NSW notes that it must be drained after commissioning or testing. Water left in the pipes can make the system non-compliant.

![Hydrant and sprinkler flow test in progress](svc18 "Flow tests check the system still delivers the water it was designed for")

### Hydrostatic (pressure) tests

A hydrostatic test fills the pipework with water under pressure and holds it there to check for leaks and weak points. Under AS 2419.1, the hydrant system design standard, every fire hydrant system must be hydrostatically tested when it's first commissioned.

After that, the hydrostatic test becomes part of the **5-yearly service**. Queensland's building regulator states that hydrostatic testing is required "for five yearly maintenance as required by AS 1851-2012".

There's one useful exception to know about. Fire Rescue Victoria notes that AS 1851 does not require a fire hydrant system *without* fire brigade booster connections to be routinely hydrostatically tested. Whether your system has a booster makes a real difference to its 5-yearly scope.

### Pump appliance (boost) tests

Where a hydrant system has a fire brigade booster, the fire brigade can connect a pumping appliance and push water into the building's system at higher pressure. The pump appliance test checks the system can handle that boost. Like the hydrostatic test, it's required on commissioning and again as part of the **5-yearly service** under AS 1851-2012.

Fire and Rescue NSW also says a booster assembly's allowable operating pressure must be at least the system's maximum design boost pressure. That's one reason the booster gets close attention during these tests.

## What about fire pumps?

Many buildings rely on on-site fire pumps (diesel or electric) to give hydrants and sprinklers enough pressure, especially taller buildings or sites where the mains supply isn't enough on its own. Fire and Rescue NSW notes that where the mains supply is insufficient, on-site water storage and fire pumps may be needed.

AS 1851 includes service schedules for fire pumps as well. If your building has them, ask your contractor to show you the pump servicing records along with the hydrant and sprinkler results, so you can see the whole system is being looked after.

## Planning for the 5-yearly service

The 5-yearly tests take more time and coordination than a routine visit. A few practical tips:

1. **Know which year you're in.** Ask your contractor when the last 5-yearly service was done, and keep the report with your building's fire safety records.
2. **Plan for disruption.** Testing at higher pressures and flowing water can affect car parks, driveways and common areas, so give residents or tenants notice.
3. **Expect follow-up work.** Pressure tests can reveal leaks or worn components. It's better to find these during a test than during a fire. Profire carries out [hydrant and sprinkler pump servicing, 5-yearly hydrant overhauls and repairs](/fire-equipment-repairs/).
4. **Check the right accreditation.** Sprinkler and hydrant systems are measures where Level 1 and Level 2 accreditation applies, so make sure whoever assesses them for your AFSS is accredited for your type of system. Our guide to [Level 1 vs Level 2 fire safety measures](/blog/level-1-vs-level-2-fire-safety-measures/) explains the difference.

## How testing connects to your AFSS

Each year, the building owner must issue an **annual fire safety statement** (AFSS) confirming that an accredited practitioner has assessed each essential fire safety measure. NSW Planning says a copy must go to the local council and the Commissioner of Fire and Rescue NSW, and FRNSW says the statement and the current fire safety schedule must be prominently displayed in the building.

Up-to-date hydrant and sprinkler test records make that assessment far simpler. Gaps in the testing history, or tests that are overdue, are much easier to sort out before your [AFSS inspection](/afss-inspections/) than after it.
