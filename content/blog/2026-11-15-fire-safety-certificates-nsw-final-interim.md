If you've just finished building works, a fit-out or a change of use, there's one document that stands between you and moving people in: the **fire safety certificate**. In NSW, a fire safety certificate confirms that every essential fire safety measure in the new or altered building has been checked and is performing as it should. This guide explains the difference between a final and interim fire safety certificate, who is responsible for it, where it needs to go, and how it starts the clock on your annual fire safety statement.

## What is a fire safety certificate?

Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW) describes a fire safety certificate as a certificate issued at the completion of building works involving any fire safety measures. It is **required before an occupation certificate can be issued**.

Each certificate is tied to the building's **fire safety schedule**, the list of essential fire safety measures for the building and the standard each one must meet. Think sprinklers, hydrants, detection and alarm systems, exit signs, emergency lighting, fire doors and so on. The certificate confirms that each of those measures has been assessed and can perform to the standard in the schedule.

## Final vs interim fire safety certificates

FRNSW says there are two types of fire safety certificate:

- **Final fire safety certificate.** Covers each essential fire safety measure in the fire safety schedule for the **completed building**. Lake Macquarie Council notes it's usually issued with the application for a final occupation certificate, once building works or the change of use are complete.
- **Interim fire safety certificate.** Covers the same measures, but only for **the part of the building the certificate relates to**. FRNSW gives part occupation of a building as the example.

You may see these terms used differently from council to council. Burwood Council, for example, says interim fire safety certificates are no longer used because interim occupation certificates are no longer issued, and that a final fire safety certificate can instead be issued for part of a building. If you're planning to occupy a building in stages, ask your principal certifier early which certificate they need for each stage.

## Who issues the certificate, and who checks the measures?

The certificate is issued by the **building owner**. FRNSW, Lake Macquarie Council and Canada Bay Council all put that responsibility on the owner, even though the testing is done by others.

Before the owner signs, a properly qualified person must inspect and verify the performance of each fire safety measure. FRNSW notes the owner can choose who assesses each measure, and that it's typically someone from the fire protection company that installed that system.

Timing matters too. Burwood and Lake Macquarie councils both say the assessment of the fire safety measures must be carried out **within three months before** the date the certificate is issued. If the job drags on and the testing gets stale, it may need to be done again.

## Where does the certificate need to go?

Once the certificate is issued, there are three places it needs to end up.

### 1. Fire and Rescue NSW

The owner must give a copy of the certificate to FRNSW as soon as possible after it's issued, along with a copy of the current fire safety schedule. FRNSW asks for both to be emailed to **firesafety@fire.nsw.gov.au**, and says there's no charge to lodge a fire safety certificate.

### 2. Council

According to FRNSW, council receives a copy once the principal certifier has determined the occupation certificate. Canada Bay Council says a certificate is required to be submitted to council before a new building, or part of a building, is occupied. Check your council's website, as some provide their own certificate template.

### 3. On display in the building

A copy of the certificate, together with a current copy of the fire safety schedule, must be **prominently displayed in the building**. A common spot is near the main entrance or the fire indicator panel, where occupants and inspectors can find it easily.

## Buildings with performance solutions: the final fire safety report

Some buildings meet fire safety requirements through a **performance solution** (an engineered design rather than the standard deemed-to-satisfy approach). For these, there's an extra step.

FRNSW explains that for building work under a construction certificate that involves a performance solution for a fire safety requirement in a Class 2 to 9 building, the certifier must request a **final fire safety report** from FRNSW and consider its findings before issuing an occupation certificate. FRNSW inspects the building and aims to provide the report to the certifier within 10 working days. The report looks at matters such as whether the works comply with the performance solution and whether hydrants are accessible for firefighters.

FRNSW also notes this report isn't required for development under a complying development certificate, and that applications for an occupation certificate should be refused if building works are incomplete. Build that inspection time into your handover program.

## How the certificate leads to your first AFSS

The fire safety certificate is the start of an ongoing obligation, not the end of one. Canada Bay Council explains that every 12 months after the fire safety certificate is issued, an **annual fire safety statement** must be prepared and forwarded to council. In other words, the date on your certificate sets the timing of your first AFSS.

That's why it pays to keep the certificate, the fire safety schedule and the commissioning records together from day one. Your [AFSS inspection](/afss-inspections/) will assess the same measures against the same schedule, and regular [routine fire inspections](/routine-fire-inspections/) in between help make sure nothing slips before the statement is due.

## A quick checklist for owners

1. **Get a copy of the fire safety schedule** from your certifier and check it lists every measure installed.
2. **Book assessments of each measure** close to handover, so they fall within three months of the certificate's date.
3. **Confirm with your certifier** whether you need a certificate for the whole building or for part of it.
4. **Issue the certificate**, then email it with the schedule to FRNSW.
5. **Display both documents** prominently in the building.
6. **Diarise your first AFSS** for 12 months after the certificate date.
