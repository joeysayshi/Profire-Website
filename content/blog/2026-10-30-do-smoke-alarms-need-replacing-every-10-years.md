If a smoke alarm still beeps when you press the test button, it's tempting to think it's fine. So do smoke alarms really need replacing every 10 years? The short answer is yes. Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW) advises replacing every type of smoke alarm at least every 10 years, and for rental properties in NSW it's a legal requirement. This guide explains why, how to work out how old your alarms are, and how strata managers and building owners can plan **smoke alarm replacement** across a whole building.

## Why smoke alarms don't last forever

FRNSW puts it simply: smoke alarms don't last forever, and the sensitivity of all smoke alarms reduces over time. An older alarm can still make a noise when you test it while being slower to pick up real smoke.

That's the key point about the test button. Pressing it checks that the battery and the alarm's sounder are working. It doesn't tell you whether the sensor is still as sensitive as it was when the alarm was new. Age is the thing a monthly test can't measure, which is why replacement is based on the date rather than on whether the alarm seems to work.

## What the rules say in NSW

Every home in NSW where people sleep must have working smoke alarms that meet Australian Standard AS 3786, with at least one on each level. Those requirements sit in Part 13 of the *Environmental Planning and Assessment (Development Certification and Fire Safety) Regulation 2021*.

For **rental properties**, the 10-year rule is written into landlords' obligations. According to NSW Government guidance, landlords must:

- **Replace smoke alarms within 10 years of manufacture**, or earlier if the manufacturer specifies a shorter life.
- **Check alarms every year** to make sure they're working.
- **Replace removable batteries every year**, or for lithium batteries, in the period the manufacturer specifies.
- **Repair or replace a faulty alarm within two business days** of finding out it isn't working.
- **Use a licensed electrician** to repair or replace a hard-wired alarm.

Landlords can't hand these responsibilities to tenants through the tenancy agreement. In strata buildings there's an exception where the owners corporation takes responsibility for repairs and replacements and certain conditions are met. We covered that in detail in our post on [smoke alarm rules for NSW strata apartments](/blog/smoke-alarm-rules-nsw-strata-apartments/).

For owner-occupied homes, the 10-year figure is FRNSW's safety advice rather than a specific replacement deadline in the regulation. But the requirement to have *working* alarms applies to everyone, and an alarm past its service life is one you can no longer rely on.

## How to tell how old a smoke alarm is

AS 3786 requires a serial or batch number on every smoke alarm. FRNSW says the batch number or date is usually on the **base of the alarm, near the battery compartment**.

A few things to watch for:

- **Some alarms show an expiry date** instead of a manufacture date. Read the label carefully so you don't add another 10 years by mistake.
- **The 10 years runs from manufacture**, not installation. An alarm that sat in a storeroom before it was fitted is already part-way through its life.
- **No visible date?** If you can't find a date or batch number, or the label is unreadable, treat the alarm as due for replacement.

Hard-wired alarms are connected to mains power, so only a licensed electrician should remove one to check the label.

## What about 10-year lithium alarms?

Many newer alarms come with a built-in 10-year lithium battery. FRNSW recommends them because the battery lasts as long as the alarm. The battery can't be replaced, so when it reaches the end of its life the whole unit is replaced.

Some of these alarms will chirp or show a warning when they reach end of life. Don't rely on that alone: check the date on the label, and follow the manufacturer's stated life if it's shorter than 10 years.

Alarms with removable batteries still need a new battery every year. FRNSW suggests using the end of daylight saving, when the clocks go back, as a reminder.

## Choosing replacements

When alarms are replaced, it's a good chance to improve on the minimum:

- **Make sure every new alarm complies with AS 3786.** It should be marked on the packaging.
- **Consider hard-wired, interconnected alarms.** FRNSW recommends these wherever possible, because when one alarm detects smoke, they all sound.
- **Check the location.** Alarms placed too close to kitchens or bathrooms tend to cause nuisance alarms, which tempts people to disable them.

In apartment buildings, smoke alarms are sometimes part of the building's fire safety measures and connected to other systems. Before replacing alarms like-for-like, check the building's fire safety schedule and talk to your fire safety contractor, so replacements don't affect how the system works or what's covered on your [annual fire safety statement](/afss-inspections/).

## Planning replacements across a strata building

Strata buildings have one challenge individual homes don't: alarms are often installed at the same time, so they reach the end of their life at the same time. A few steps make that much easier to manage:

1. **Build an alarm register.** Record each lot or area, the alarm type (battery, lithium or hard-wired), and the manufacture or expiry date.
2. **Confirm who is responsible.** Check the fire safety schedule and the scheme's records, and make sure lot owners and landlords know whether the owners corporation handles in-unit alarms.
3. **Schedule replacements in advance.** Knowing the dates lets you plan access, budget and electrician time rather than reacting to failures.
4. **Coordinate access.** Replacing alarms in occupied apartments means giving residents and tenants notice. Grouping work by floor or building helps.
5. **Keep records.** Note what was replaced, when and by whom. It makes questions from owners, tenants and your AFSS practitioner easy to answer.
