When people think about fire safety, they usually picture sprinklers, alarms and extinguishers. But much of what keeps a building safe in a fire is built into the walls, floors and ceilings themselves. This is **passive fire protection**, and the small details, such as how a pipe passes through a fire-rated wall, can decide whether it works. This guide explains passive fire protection, what penetrations and fire collars are, and what NSW building owners and strata managers need to know about maintaining them.

## Passive vs active fire protection

Fire safety measures generally fall into two groups. The NSW Government's Regulatory Impact Statement for the fire safety regulations contrasts **passive** measures, such as fire-rated walls, with **active** measures, such as fire sprinkler systems.

- **Active measures** do something when a fire starts: detectors sense smoke, alarms sound, sprinklers discharge, pumps start.
- **Passive measures** are part of the building's construction. Fire-rated walls and floors, fire doors, fire dampers, fire shutters and fire seals are designed to hold back fire and smoke for a set period, without needing to switch on.

Together, passive measures divide a building into fire-resisting compartments. In an apartment building, that compartmentation is what helps stop a fire in one unit spreading to the next, and gives people time to get out.

## What is a service penetration?

Buildings are full of services: plumbing, electrical cabling, data, gas and air conditioning. To get from one part of the building to another, these services often have to pass through fire-rated walls and floors. Each opening made for them is a **service penetration**.

A guidance note on service penetrations from the Victorian Building Authority explains the risk simply: if a penetration through a fire-rated or smoke-proof wall is installed incorrectly, it can create a weak point that lets fire and smoke spread. A wall that would otherwise hold back a fire can be undone by one unsealed hole.

That's why the list of fire safety measures used in NSW includes "fire seals protecting openings in fire-resisting components of the building", alongside fire doors, fire dampers and fire shutters.

## Fire collars, sealants and wraps

There isn't one product that suits every penetration. The right method depends on the type of service, the material it's made of and the fire resistance level of the wall or floor. Common methods include:

- **Fire collars.** A collar fitted around a pipe or conduit, containing intumescent material that expands under heat. They're commonly used on plastic pipes, which soften and burn away in a fire. As the pipe melts, the collar's material swells and closes off the opening.
- **Fire-rated sealants (fire mastic).** Used to seal gaps around cables, pipes and joints.
- **Wraps.** Flexible intumescent strips wrapped around pipes or conduits.
- **Other systems**, such as fire pillows, fire mortar, fire-rated boards and fire dampers in air-conditioning ductwork.

In the National Construction Code, penetrations of fire-resisting elements are covered by provisions in Volume One, and the Australian Standard **AS 4072.1** deals with testing and installing penetration sealing systems. In practice, this means a seal or collar should be a tested system that matches the service and the wall or floor it passes through. Not every product is suitable for every penetration.

## Why penetrations get compromised

Passive fire protection is often installed correctly when a building is new, then slowly compromised over the years. The NSW Government's Regulatory Impact Statement noted that common defects in multi-unit residential buildings include defective or missing fire safety measures, "especially for passive systems", and listed problems such as missing fire barriers and incomplete fire door and seal work in other buildings.

Common ways this happens include:

- **New cabling and data.** Installers drill through a fire-rated wall or ceiling to run new cables and don't reseal the hole.
- **Renovations and plumbing work.** Pipes are replaced or rerouted, and the old collar is removed or the new pipe isn't protected.
- **Mismatched products.** A general-purpose filler or foam is used instead of a tested fire-rated system.
- **Hidden areas.** Penetrations sit in risers, ceiling spaces and plant rooms that few people check.

Because passive protection doesn't "do" anything day to day, problems usually stay invisible until an inspection, or a fire.

## What NSW building owners need to know

**Owners must maintain passive measures too.** Under section 81 of the *Environmental Planning and Assessment (Development Certification and Fire Safety) Regulation 2021*, the owner must keep each essential fire safety measure maintained to the standard in the building's fire safety schedule. As Hornsby Shire Council puts it, this places ultimate responsibility for maintaining fire safety measures on the building owner. Fire seals, fire doors and other passive measures listed in your schedule are part of that duty.

**They're part of your annual fire safety statement.** Each year, the essential fire safety measures in your schedule must be assessed by an accredited practitioner (fire safety) before the [annual fire safety statement](/afss-inspections/) is issued. If fire seals are on your schedule, they need to be checked as part of that process.

**New building work is inspected.** For new buildings and certain building work in Class 2, 3 and 4 buildings, which include apartment buildings, the Regulation requires an inspection of fire protection at service penetrations before it's covered up.

**Tell contractors before they drill.** If a tradesperson needs to run services through a fire-rated wall or floor, make sure the penetration is resealed with a suitable fire-rated system and that you get a record of what was done. Strata managers may want to make this a standard condition when approving works in common areas.
