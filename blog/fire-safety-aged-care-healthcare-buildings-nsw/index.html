Fire Safety in Aged Care and Healthcare Buildings in NSW

Why aged care homes and hospitals have stricter fire safety rules in NSW, from mandatory sprinklers to smoke compartments, and how to keep them compliant.

8 November 2026 · Profire Australia

Most buildings are designed on the assumption that people will get themselves out when the alarm sounds. Aged care homes and hospitals can't make that assumption. Many residents and patients need help to move, some can't be moved quickly at all, and staff numbers drop overnight. That's why fire safety in aged care and healthcare buildings in NSW is held to a higher standard than in an office block or apartment building. This guide explains what's different, where the rules come from, and what owners and facility managers need to keep on top of.

How the building code treats aged care and healthcare

The National Construction Code (NCC) sorts buildings into classes. Health-care buildings, such as hospitals, are Class 9a. Residential care buildings, such as aged care homes, are Class 9c. Each class triggers its own set of fire safety requirements.

The Australian Building Codes Board's guide to clause C3D6 sums up the intent plainly: the provisions exist "to protect patients in a health-care building and residents in an aged care building from the spread of fire and smoke."

In practice, that means splitting the building into smaller areas so a fire or smoke stays where it starts, and staff can move people a short distance to safety instead of evacuating the whole building at once.

Fire safety in aged care and healthcare: compartments and smoke walls

The ABCB guide explains how C3D6 works for each class:

  • Class 9a (health-care): the building needs fire compartments, plus sub-compartments to allow staged evacuation. The guide notes a 2,000 m² maximum for patient care areas, and that wards must be subdivided into areas of no more than 1,000 m² by walls with a fire resistance level (FRL) of 60/60/60.
  • Class 9c (residential care): the building must be divided into areas of no more than 500 m² by smoke-proof walls. The guide explains that no further subdivision is needed because Class 9c buildings must be sprinkler protected.

These smoke-proof walls are set out in NCC Specification 11. Their job is to create a "smoke compartment" that stops or limits smoke spreading into the next area. The specification also requires every penetration through the walls and ceiling of a smoke compartment to be sealed against smoke. Even a light fitting in a flush plasterboard ceiling counts.

That last point matters long after the building is finished. Every time a cable, pipe or duct is run through a smoke wall during a renovation or IT upgrade, the seal needs to be put back properly. Gaps left behind by trades are one of the easiest ways for a compliant building to quietly become non-compliant.

NSW's sprinkler requirement for aged care homes

NSW went further than the national code after the 2011 Quakers Hill nursing home fire. According to NSW Planning, the Government made automatic fire sprinklers mandatory in all residential aged care facilities, with the laws taking effect on 1 January 2013.

The department's advisory note on the new requirements explained that:

  • Approved providers of residential care facilities under the Commonwealth Aged Care Act 1997 had to install sprinklers in any building containing a resident area, where a fully functional system wasn't already in place at 1 January 2013. This included Class 3 and 9a buildings within those facilities.
  • From 1 January 2013, new Class 3 or 9a buildings (or new parts) with a resident area in a seniors residential care facility also had to be sprinklered.
  • The requirements did not apply to retirement villages, group homes, multipurpose facilities or transition care.

Existing facilities were given time to retrofit, with completion dates of 1 September 2014 or 1 March 2016 depending on the option chosen. NSW Planning now reports that all aged care homes in the retrofit program have completed the work and are sprinkler protected.

The department's Fire Sprinkler Standard sets the technical rules for these systems. Among other things, it calls for residential sprinkler heads in resident areas where the design is based on AS 2118.1 or AS 2118.6, a monitored stop valve, and a permanent direct data link to a Fire and Rescue NSW authorised alarm monitoring provider.

Installation is only the start

A sprinkler system protects residents only if it works on the day it's needed. The advisory note made this clear: once installed, these sprinkler systems became essential fire safety measures, subject to the same ongoing maintenance and certification rules as any other.

For aged care and healthcare operators, that means:

  1. Routine maintenance to AS1851. Sprinklers, pumps, detection, alarm and warning systems, exit signs, emergency lighting, fire doors and smoke walls all need regular inspection and testing. Our routine fire inspections cover these measures.
  2. An Annual Fire Safety Statement (AFSS). The building owner must certify each year that every essential fire safety measure on the fire safety schedule has been assessed by a competent fire safety practitioner and is performing to its standard. See our AFSS inspections page for how the process works.
  3. Prompt repairs. A faulty smoke door closer, an isolated sprinkler valve or a broken seal in a smoke wall weakens the compartment strategy the whole building relies on. Our fire equipment repairs team can fix defects found during testing.
  4. Emergency planning. AS 4083 is the Australian Standard for planning for emergencies in health care facilities. A good plan reflects how the building is compartmented, so staff know where to move people first.

Practical tips for facility managers

  • Know your fire safety schedule. It lists every measure your building relies on, and it's what your AFSS is assessed against.
  • Control contractor work. Ask trades to tell you before they penetrate any fire or smoke wall, and check the seals afterwards.
  • Keep smoke doors clear. Wedges, furniture and linen trolleys holding doors open defeat the purpose of the compartment.
  • Plan testing around residents. Alarm and sprinkler tests in occupied care buildings need coordination with staff so residents aren't distressed and the monitoring service is notified.
  • Keep records together. Photographic defect reports and maintenance logs make it far easier to show compliance and track repairs.
