Why Your Fire Panel Is Beeping (and When to Call a Technician)

Fire panel beeping in your building? Learn what fire, fault and isolate indications usually mean, what's safe to do and when to call a fire technician.

1 November 2026 · Profire Australia

It usually starts with a call from a resident or a tenant: "There's a box in the foyer that won't stop beeping." If you manage a strata building, office or commercial site in NSW, sooner or later you'll hear about a fire panel beeping. Most of the time it's the panel telling you something needs attention rather than a fire, but it's never something to ignore. This guide explains what the panel is, what its beeps and lights usually mean, what's safe for you to do, and when to call a fire technician.

What is a fire indicator panel?

The fire indicator panel (often called the FIP, or a fire alarm control panel) is the brain of your building's fire detection system. It's typically in the main entry, foyer or fire control room. It watches every smoke and heat detector, manual call point and connected device, and it starts the building alarm when one of them activates.

In many NSW buildings, the alarm system is also monitored by an automatic fire alarm service provider, so a fire alarm can be passed to Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW) automatically.

The panel also checks itself. When it finds a problem with its power, wiring or devices, it lights an indicator and sounds its internal buzzer to get someone's attention. That buzzer is the beeping most people hear.

Fire panel beeping: what the sounds and lights usually mean

Every panel model is a little different, so check the labels on yours and the operating instructions your service provider left on site. As an example, the installation manual for the Ampac FireFinder Plus, a panel built to the Australian AS 7240 series, describes these main conditions:

  • Fire. The FIRE indicator lights and the buzzer sounds a steady tone. The fire condition stays latched until it's manually reset.
  • Fault. The FAULT indicator lights and the buzzer sounds an unsteady (intermittent) tone. On that panel, faults clear themselves once the cause is fixed.
  • Disabled or isolated. A DISABLED/ISOLATED indicator shows that part of the system has been switched off, for example a detector or zone isolated during building works.
  • Power. The SUPPLY indicator shows the panel has mains power. On that model it flashes when mains has failed and the panel is running on its backup batteries.

So a steady tone with a fire light means treat it as a fire alarm. An on-and-off beep with a fault light usually means the system has found a problem it needs you to know about.

Common reasons for a fault beep

The same Ampac manual lists the kinds of faults the panel reports. These are typical of many panels:

  • Mains power problems, such as a power outage, a tripped circuit or a charger fault. The panel runs on batteries, but they won't last forever.
  • Battery problems, such as low battery voltage or a missing battery. Backup batteries age and need replacing as part of routine maintenance.
  • Wiring faults, including short circuits or open circuits on detector loops or monitored outputs. These can follow building works, water leaks or damaged cabling.
  • Earth faults, where a signal cable is shorting to earth somewhere in the building.

The manual also notes that after a short or open circuit is repaired, a loop test needs to be done to clear the fault. That's one reason fixing the cause isn't always the end of the job.

What's safe to do when the panel is beeping

If it's a fire alarm

Treat it as real. Follow your building's evacuation procedures, and in an emergency call Triple Zero (000). Don't try to reset the panel to stop the alarm. The cause needs to be checked first, and on a monitored system the alarm may already have gone through to FRNSW.

If it's a fault

  • Read the panel's display or indicators and write down exactly what it says, including any zone or device number.
  • Silence the buzzer only if your panel allows it at your access level. On the Ampac panel, the Silence Buzzer button quietens only the panel's own buzzer. It doesn't fix the fault, and the fault indicator stays on. Silencing building alarms is a separate control at a higher access level.
  • Check for obvious causes like a power outage in the building or works happening near detectors, and tell your fire service provider.
  • Don't isolate detectors, change settings or reset the panel unless you've been trained to and your service provider has told you to. Those controls exist for technicians, and the Ampac manual says that if in doubt, contact the supplier.

Silencing a fault buzzer and walking away is the risky option. A fault can mean part of your building isn't protected until it's repaired.

When to call a fire technician

Call your fire service provider promptly if:

  • The fault indicator stays on, or the beeping comes back after it's silenced.
  • The panel shows a wiring, earth or device fault.
  • The panel is running on battery because of a mains failure that isn't a short, building-wide blackout.
  • Part of the system has been isolated and nobody is sure why, or for how long.
  • You've had repeat unwanted alarms. FRNSW's guidance for building owners lists system malfunctions and poorly maintained systems among the causes of unwanted alarms, and recommends systems be tested and maintained to the relevant Australian Standards by a professional fire alarm technician.

Why it matters for building owners

In NSW, building owners must maintain each essential fire safety measure in their building, including automatic fire alarm systems. FRNSW's guideline on alarm monitoring summarises this duty under the Environmental Planning and Assessment (Development Certification and Fire Safety) Regulation 2021: each measure must be maintained to the standard in the building's fire safety schedule or, if there's no schedule, to a standard no lower than it was originally designed to meet.

A panel left in fault or with zones isolated for weeks can cause problems at your next annual fire safety statement, and it can mean a fire isn't detected as quickly as it should be. Unwanted alarms can also lead to FRNSW attendance charges for whoever holds the alarm monitoring contract.

Regular routine fire inspections under AS1851 help catch ageing batteries, wiring problems and faulty detectors before they turn into a beeping panel at 2am.
