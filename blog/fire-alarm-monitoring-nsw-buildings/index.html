Fire Alarm Monitoring in NSW: How Your Building's Alarm Reaches Fire and Rescue NSW

How fire alarm monitoring works in NSW, which buildings must be monitored, who the approved providers are, and what owners need to manage along the way.

16 November 2026 · Profire Australia

When a smoke detector activates in a monitored building, a fire truck can be on its way before anyone has picked up a phone. That link between your fire panel and Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW) is called fire alarm monitoring, and in NSW it runs through a small group of contracted providers. This guide explains how fire alarm monitoring in NSW works, which buildings need it, and what strata managers and building owners are responsible for.

How fire alarm monitoring works in NSW

An automatic fire alarm (AFA) system detects smoke or heat and raises the alarm in the building. In a monitored building, the system also sends that alarm off site so firefighters are dispatched automatically.

FRNSW doesn't take these signals straight from each building. Its website says AFA systems that require monitoring must be connected to an approved Automatic Fire Alarm Service Provider (AFASP), and that there are four approved AFASPs contracted to FRNSW. The AFASP receives the signal from your building and passes it to FRNSW, which has a statutory obligation under the Fire and Rescue NSW Act 1989 to attend all fire alarms.

At the time of writing, FRNSW lists these four approved providers:

  • Chubb Fire Safety Ltd
  • Johnson Controls Australia Pty Ltd
  • Romteck Grid Pty Ltd
  • Security 1 Pty Ltd

Check FRNSW's Automatic Fire Alarm Service Providers page for the current list and contact numbers before you sign or change a contract.

Which buildings need fire alarm monitoring?

FRNSW's AFASP page refers to the National Construction Code's requirements for smoke detection systems. It lists the following situations where monitoring is required:

  • smoke detection in a Class 3 building (for example hotels, motels, boarding houses and backpackers)
  • smoke detection in a Class 9a health-care building with more than 20 patients
  • smoke detection in a Class 9c building (residential aged care)
  • smoke detection that activates a smoke exhaust system or smoke-and-heat vents

FRNSW also notes that other buildings may be monitored because they have a sprinkler system, because it's a condition of their development consent, because their insurer requires it, or simply because the owner chooses to.

The easiest way to check your own building is to look at its fire safety schedule. If alarm monitoring is listed there as an essential fire safety measure, it has to be maintained and included in your annual fire safety statement.

Connecting a new system

FRNSW says that before a system is commissioned, the building owner (via the system installer) must connect it with a current approved AFASP. Connection and testing must happen before commissioning and before the relevant fire safety certificate is issued. The connection to FRNSW's dispatch centre is covered by Australian Standard AS 1670.3, which deals with fire alarm monitoring.

If you're managing a new building or a major upgrade, it's worth confirming early who is arranging the AFASP connection, so it doesn't hold up the fire safety certificate at the end of the job.

Making sure firefighters can find the panel

When crews arrive, they need to find the fire panel quickly. FRNSW's guideline on alarm installation locations says it must be able to identify the building's designated entry point. The fire indicator panel must be visible and accessible there, or, if the panel is somewhere else, a mimic or repeater panel must be installed at that entry point.

So if you're renovating a foyer, changing the main entrance or moving the panel, talk to your fire contractor first. A change that seems minor can affect how quickly firefighters get to the information they need.

Who pays when there's no fire?

Monitored buildings can receive a charge when FRNSW attends a false alarm that the owner could reasonably have prevented. FRNSW invoices the AFASP, which then invoices the building owner or manager, and FRNSW says the charge sits with whoever holds the contract with the AFASP. In a strata building, that's usually the owners corporation.

There are leniency rules and a waiver process, which we cover in detail in our guide to false fire alarm charges in NSW. FRNSW's waiver guideline also notes that an AFASP may add its own administration surcharge, which must be shown on the invoice.

Can you stop monitoring?

Not by simply cancelling the contract. FRNSW's guideline on ceasing monitoring says the request is made by the AFASP, not the owner. The AFASP must give written notice to the local council and then apply to FRNSW, including the reason, the proposed disconnection date and the relevant development consent. The AFASP has to keep monitoring the system until FRNSW advises otherwise.

The guideline lists non-payment of the provider's accounts among the reasons an AFASP can apply to cease monitoring. If monitoring is a required measure for your building, letting the account lapse can leave you with a council compliance issue as well as a building that's no longer protected.

Practical tips for strata managers and owners

  1. Know your provider. Keep the AFASP's name, contract details and your building's alarm details with your fire safety records.
  2. Check the fire safety schedule. Confirm whether monitoring is listed as an essential fire safety measure.
  3. Keep the contract current. Make sure invoices are paid and the contract is renewed on time.
  4. Coordinate testing. Routine testing of alarms and pumps can involve isolating the system. Make sure your contractor manages isolations properly and that residents know when testing is happening.
  5. Register contractors on site. FRNSW says all workers on site must register with the building owner or manager, and isolations of the alarm system need to be managed, so dust and heat from works don't trigger a call-out.
  6. Act on faults quickly. A panel left in fault can mean a fire isn't detected or reported as quickly as it should be. Our guide to why your fire panel is beeping explains what the common signals mean.
