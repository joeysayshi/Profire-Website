Fire Extinguisher Types: Which Ones Does Your Building Need?

A plain guide to fire extinguisher types and classes of fire in Australia, and how NSW buildings work out which extinguishers they need and where.

2 November 2026 · Profire Australia

Most buildings have at least one fire extinguisher on the wall, but few people could say which type it is or whether it's the right one for the room it's in. That matters, because using the wrong extinguisher on some fires can make things worse. This guide explains the main fire extinguisher types used in Australia, the classes of fire they're designed for, and how NSW building owners and strata managers work out which ones their building needs.

The classes of fire

Extinguishers are matched to the kind of fuel that's burning. Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW) groups fires into these classes:

  • Class A: ordinary solid combustibles such as paper, cardboard and clothing.
  • Class B: flammable liquids, including petroleum products (petrol, oil, grease) and polar solvents (alcohols, spirits).
  • Class C: flammable gases such as LPG, natural gas and acetylene.
  • Class D: combustible metals.
  • Class E: fires involving energised electrical equipment.
  • Class F: cooking oils and fats.

One point FRNSW stresses for Class C: don't put out a gas fire until the gas supply has been shut off.

The main fire extinguisher types

In Australia, extinguishers are red, with a coloured band that shows what's inside. Queensland Fire and Emergency Services summarises the colour coding like this:

  • Red band – water. For Class A fires only.
  • Blue band – foam. For Class A and B fires.
  • White band – dry chemical powder. ABE powder covers Class A, B and electrical fires; BE powder covers Class B and electrical fires.
  • Black band – carbon dioxide (CO2). For electrical fires, with some use on Class B fires.
  • Yellow band – vaporising liquid. For electrical fires and some Class A and B fires.
  • Oatmeal band – wet chemical. Designed for Class F cooking oil and fat fires.

FRNSW's guidance lines up with this. For example, it lists wet chemical, BE powder and a fire blanket as suitable for cooking oil fires, and BE or ABE powder, CO2 and vaporising liquid for energised electrical equipment. Water is not on its list for either.

Class D metal fires need a special powder, and are only a consideration in specialised workplaces.

Which extinguishers does your building need?

There's no single answer, because it depends on the building and what happens in it. Three things decide it.

1. The building's approval and fire safety schedule

In NSW, the extinguishers your building needs are often set when it's approved and listed on its fire safety schedule. The National Construction Code (NCC) clause on portable fire extinguishers, E1D14, requires them to be installed in line with Sections 1 to 4 of AS 2444, the Australian Standard for selecting and locating portable fire extinguishers and fire blankets.

For apartment buildings (Class 2) and other residential buildings such as hotels (Class 3), the Australian Building Codes Board's guide to the NCC explains that extinguishers are required where the building has internal fire hydrants, or for any fire compartment larger than 500 m². In these buildings, each sole-occupancy unit counts as a fire compartment. The NCC also sets specific requirements for the type, size and location of extinguishers in Class 2 and 3 buildings.

Your fire safety schedule is the best place to start. If you can't find yours, your council or fire safety practitioner can help.

2. The hazards in each area

FRNSW advises distributing extinguishers according to the hazards present, not just the floor area. In practice that means thinking room by room:

  • Corridors and general areas mostly hold Class A materials such as paper, furniture and fabric.
  • Commercial kitchens and café kitchens carry a cooking oil and fat risk, which calls for an extinguisher and fire blanket suited to Class F fires.
  • Switch rooms and electrical areas need an extinguisher that's safe on energised equipment, such as CO2 or powder.
  • Plant rooms, car parks and workshops may store fuels, oils or solvents, which are Class B risks.

3. Changes to the building

If you renovate, fit out a new tenancy or change how a space is used, the hazards can change too. FRNSW notes that you should get council approval before changing your building's scheduled fire safety measures, and suggests contacting your council's fire safety officer. Don't swap, remove or relocate extinguishers on your schedule without advice.

Where extinguishers should be located

FRNSW's guidance for businesses says extinguishers should be:

  • In conspicuous, readily accessible locations, with at least 1,000 mm of clear space around them.
  • Mounted on a bracket or in a cabinet, facing outwards, with the bottom at least 100 mm above the floor and the top of the handle no higher than 1,200 mm.
  • Marked with a sign (white symbol and lettering on a red background) at least 2,000 mm above the floor, visible from up to 20 metres away.
  • Protected from heat or cold where temperatures fall outside 5°C to 50°C.

The ABCB guide adds that extinguishers should be in plain view, along normal paths of travel and, where possible, near exits.

Keeping extinguishers in working order

An extinguisher that hasn't been looked after may not work when it's needed. In NSW, the building owner is responsible for maintaining each fire safety measure to its required standard of performance, and councils note that owners can't hand this duty off to an agent. Where extinguishers are listed on your fire safety schedule, they're assessed as part of your annual fire safety statement.

Between inspections, it's worth checking that extinguishers are still in place, not blocked by furniture or stock, and haven't been discharged or tampered with. Routine testing and servicing to AS 1851 is a job for a qualified technician, and our routine fire inspections come with a photographic report so you can see the condition of each item. If an extinguisher is missing, damaged or out of date, we can repair or replace it.
