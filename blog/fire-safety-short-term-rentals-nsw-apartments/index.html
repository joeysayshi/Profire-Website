Fire Safety Rules for Short-Term Rentals in NSW Apartments

Letting an apartment on Airbnb or Stayz in NSW? Here's what the short-term rental fire safety standard requires, from smoke alarms to evacuation diagrams.

11 November 2026 · Profire Australia

Summer is the busiest time of year for holiday lets, and plenty of NSW apartments double as short-stay accommodation. If you own one, manage a building that has some, or sit on a strata committee fielding questions about them, it helps to know the fire safety rules for short-term rentals in NSW. They're set out in a state-wide fire safety standard, and they apply on top of the rules for ordinary residential living.

Here's what the standard requires inside an apartment, how it differs for houses, and how it fits with the rest of your building's fire safety.

Why short-term rentals have their own fire safety standard

NSW's framework for short-term rental accommodation (STRA) came into effect on 1 November 2021. Under it, all hosts must first make sure the dwelling complies with the fire and safety requirements and agree to follow the code of conduct, and every STRA property must be registered on the Government-run STRA Register.

The reason for a separate standard is simple. Its stated objective is the safety of people staying in short-term rental accommodation, "who may be less familiar with the dwelling". A guest arriving late on a Friday night doesn't know where the exits are, where the extinguisher lives or how the building's alarm sounds.

The standard applies to all dwellings providing short-term rental accommodation. It has two sets of requirements:

  • Class 1a dwellings: detached houses, town houses, row houses and the like.
  • Class 2 and Class 4 dwellings: apartments, and a sole dwelling within a non-residential building (such as a flat above a shop).

Most strata units fall into the second group, so that's where we'll focus.

STRA fire safety requirements for NSW apartments

For an apartment (Class 2) or Class 4 dwelling, the standard covers five main things.

1. Smoke alarms

Smoke alarms must be installed on or near the ceiling:

  • in every corridor or hallway associated with a bedroom, or, if there's no corridor or hallway, in an area between the bedrooms and the rest of the dwelling, and
  • on every other storey of the dwelling that doesn't contain bedrooms.

The alarms must comply with AS 3786 and be powered either by mains electricity or by a non-removable battery with a life expectancy of at least 10 years. If there's more than one alarm in the dwelling, they must be interconnected. The NSW Government's guide for hosts confirms that wireless interconnected smoke alarms are permitted.

This is worth checking carefully. Many older apartments still have a single replaceable-battery alarm, which won't meet the standard.

2. A fire extinguisher in the kitchen

A 2.5 kg ABE portable fire extinguisher must be installed in an accessible location in the kitchen, in accordance with Section 3 of AS 2444.

3. A fire blanket in the kitchen

A fire blanket must also be installed in an accessible location in the kitchen, in accordance with Section 6 of AS 2444.

4. An entrance door that opens without a key

The apartment's entrance door must be able to be opened from inside without a key. Any change to the lock must not impair the fire resistance of the door. This matters in strata buildings, where unit entry doors are often fire-rated. Swapping in a smart lock or adding a deadbolt without checking can create a fire door defect as well as an exit problem.

5. Evacuation diagrams

An evacuation diagram must be fixed inside the dwelling on or next to the entrance door, and in each bedroom on or next to the bedroom door. Each diagram must:

  • be mounted between 1,200 mm and 1,600 mm above the floor
  • be at least A4, clear, legible and durable, and show its date of issue
  • carry the title "EVACUATION DIAGRAM", a "YOU ARE HERE" indicator, the property address and a legend
  • show exits and paths of travel in green, and fire equipment such as hose reels, extinguishers and fire blankets in red
  • show the assembly area, the emergency number 000, and advice to download the Emergency+ app.

The NSW Government's guide says hosts can prepare or draw the diagrams themselves; they don't need to be done by a fire safety professional. It also says Class 2 and Class 4 buildings need signage showing where the fire safety equipment and the assembly point are.

Emergency information for guests

Separately from the diagrams, the host guide says hosts must provide an information booklet or advice sheet that's easy for guests to find in the property. It should cover hazards, how to evacuate, assembly areas and emergency contacts.

How it differs for houses and town houses

For a Class 1a dwelling, the smoke alarm and evacuation diagram rules are much the same. The standard also includes a heat alarm requirement for certain garages, and the official host guide adds its own explanation of when one is needed, so owners of houses with a garage should read both carefully. The kitchen extinguisher, fire blanket and keyless entrance door requirements in the standard apply to apartments and Class 4 dwellings.

Where the building's own fire safety fits in

The STRA standard deals with what's inside the individual dwelling. It doesn't replace the fire safety measures that protect the whole building, such as the fire alarm system, hydrants, exit signs, emergency lighting and fire doors. Those still need to be maintained and certified through the building's annual fire safety statement, regardless of how individual units are used.

It's also worth knowing that, according to NSW Planning, a strata by-law or a condition of development consent may restrict short-term rental in some residential premises. If you're unsure, NSW Planning suggests contacting your council, owners corporation or NSW Fair Trading.

A quick checklist for owners and strata managers

  1. Confirm the dwelling type. Apartment (Class 2) or house (Class 1a) decides which requirements apply.
  2. Check the smoke alarms. AS 3786 compliant, mains or 10-year sealed battery, in the right locations, and interconnected if there's more than one.
  3. Check the kitchen. A 2.5 kg ABE extinguisher and a fire blanket, both easy to reach.
  4. Test the entry door. It should open from inside without a key, and any lock work shouldn't affect a fire-rated door.
  5. Put up evacuation diagrams at the entrance and in every bedroom, at the right height.
  6. Leave an emergency information sheet where guests will see it.
  7. Register on the STRA Register and keep it renewed.

Requirements can change, so check the current rules on the NSW Planning website before you list a property.
