How to Read a Fire Safety Defect Report

Critical defect, non-critical defect or non-conformance? A plain guide to reading your fire safety defect report and deciding what to fix first in NSW.

6 November 2026 · Profire Australia

After every routine inspection, your fire contractor should hand you more than a tick in the logbook. A good fire safety defect report tells you what was tested, what passed, what failed and how urgent each problem is. But if you're a strata manager or building owner looking at pages of photos and codes, it can be hard to know where to start.

This guide explains the terms you'll see, what each one asks of you, and how to turn the report into a sensible repair plan.

Why defect reports matter more in NSW now

In NSW, building owners must ensure essential fire safety measures are inspected and tested in accordance with AS 1851-2012 (or the standard nominated in the building's performance solution). Building Commission NSW says owners must make sure maintenance is done by competent people, and that systems stay operational at all times by carrying out remedial works for identified defects promptly.

Building Commission NSW gives 13 February 2026 as the implementation date for these requirements, and expects the 1-yearly service to be completed by February 2027, regardless of the age of the building. So the reports you receive over the coming year matter more than ever.

The three outcomes in a fire safety defect report

When an inspection under AS 1851-2012 finds a problem, it's recorded as one of three types. Building Commission NSW describes them like this.

Critical defect

A critical defect renders the system inoperable and must be addressed as soon as possible. Think of it as "this system may not work if there's a fire today".

Building Commission NSW also suggests owners consider having mitigation strategies in place in case there's an emergency while the system is inoperable. What that looks like depends on the system and the building, so ask your contractor what interim measures make sense.

Non-critical defect

A non-critical defect won't critically affect how the fire safety measure operates. That doesn't mean it can be ignored. Building Commission NSW recommends owners have a plan to fix these, because they may evolve into a critical defect in the future.

Non-conformance

A non-conformance is generally caused by missing or incorrect information and doesn't affect how the system works. For example, an older building may not have access to its original designs. Building Commission NSW says non-conformances should be assessed case by case with a suitably qualified person, and accepts that resolving them may not always be possible.

Building Commission NSW also notes that the legislation doesn't require non-conformances or non-critical defects to be resolved immediately.

What about missing "baseline data"?

You may see items flagged because baseline data is missing. Baseline data is the initial set of parameters the testing is carried out against.

Many buildings, especially older ones, don't have all of it. According to Building Commission NSW, AS 1851-2012 requires very minimal baseline data, and when data is missing the item is generally noted as a non-critical defect or non-conformance rather than a critical defect. So a long list of baseline data items usually isn't a sign that your systems are failing.

How to read the report, step by step

  1. Start with critical defects. Find every item marked critical and confirm with your contractor what's needed to get it working again. Don't wait for the next committee meeting if a system is inoperable.
  2. Look at the photos. A photographic report lets you see the problem for yourself: a damaged fire door, a blocked exit, a faulty light. It also makes it easier to explain the issue to owners and the committee.
  3. Check the location and the measure. Each item should say which fire safety measure it relates to and where it is in the building, so you can match it against your fire safety schedule.
  4. Ask for the reasoning in writing. If a recommendation isn't clear, Building Commission NSW suggests asking the contractor to provide it in writing and cite the relevant clause or standard.
  5. Group the non-critical defects. Plan these into your maintenance budget, and fix the ones most likely to get worse first.
  6. Note the non-conformances. Talk them through with a qualified person and record what you decide, even if the answer is that the information can't be recovered.

Keep the paperwork

Your report sits alongside the logbook, and both matter. Building Commission NSW says:

  • AS 1851-2012 requires a hard copy logbook to be left on site after testing. An electronic record can supplement it but can't replace the physical logbook yet.
  • Owners must keep records in the manner AS 1851-2012 prescribes for at least 7 years and make them available for inspection. Councils and Fire and Rescue NSW can ask to see logbooks.
  • If you change contractors, you're expected to give the incoming contractor the existing logbook.

How defects relate to your AFSS

Routine AS 1851 maintenance and your annual fire safety statement are separate processes. However, Building Commission NSW notes that an AFSS assessor may consider the outcomes of your AS 1851 inspection results when doing their assessment.

The NSW Government's guide for building owners explains that the AFSS assessment is about whether the fire safety measures can perform to their required standards. Owners also have an ongoing duty to keep those measures in working order, because a fire can happen at any time. Leaving defects open all year and hoping to fix them just before the AFSS is due is a risky approach.
