Fire Dampers and Smoke Control Systems in NSW Buildings: What Owners Need to Know

Fire dampers and smoke control systems hide in ceilings and plant rooms. Here's what they do, why they're on your Fire Safety Schedule and how they're serviced.

13 November 2026 · Profire Australia

Most of the fire safety measures in a building are easy to spot: extinguishers on the wall, exit signs over the doors, hydrants in the car park. Fire dampers and smoke control systems are different. They sit inside ductwork, above ceilings and in plant rooms, so they're easy to forget about until an inspection report lists them as a defect. This guide explains what they are, why they appear on so many NSW Fire Safety Schedules, and what building owners and strata managers need to do to keep them compliant.

What is a fire damper?

Air-conditioning and ventilation ducts have to pass through walls and floors, including fire-rated ones. A fire-rated wall is designed to hold a fire back for a set period, but a duct running through it creates an opening that fire could travel along.

A fire damper closes that gap. The most common type has a heat-sensitive link that holds a curtain or blade open. When the link is exposed to heat it releases, the damper drops shut and seals the duct opening. Other types use intumescent material that swells when heated and fills the opening. In Australia, fire dampers are covered by the AS 1682 series of standards.

Smoke dampers and smoke control systems

Smoke is often the bigger danger to people in a fire, so many buildings also have systems designed to manage it. Depending on the building, these can include:

  • Smoke dampers, which are motorised and controlled by signals from the fire detection system rather than by heat
  • Combination fire and smoke dampers, which do both jobs in one unit
  • Stair pressurisation, which pushes air into fire stairs to keep smoke out of the escape route
  • Smoke exhaust systems, which draw smoke out of the building
  • Fire mode controls on air handling units, which shut down or change how the air-conditioning runs when the fire alarm activates

These systems are linked to the fire detection and alarm system. When a detector activates, the building's mechanical services are meant to switch into "fire mode" automatically, so that air-conditioning stops feeding smoke around the building.

Why they're on your Fire Safety Schedule

In NSW, the essential fire safety measures in your building are listed on its Fire Safety Schedule, and they're the measures your annual fire safety statement certifies. Council lists of statutory fire safety measures include fire dampers, smoke dampers and mechanical air handling systems, alongside items such as fire doors, smoke doors, fire seals and smoke and heat vents.

If they're on your schedule, they must be maintained like every other measure listed there.

How fire dampers and smoke control systems are maintained

Since 13 February 2026, Building Commission NSW says all class 1b and class 2 to 9 buildings in NSW must have their essential fire safety measures inspected and tested in accordance with AS 1851-2012 (unless a performance solution nominates another standard). That includes the mechanical fire and smoke control features in your building.

AS 1851-2012 has a section dedicated to the fire and smoke control features of mechanical services. AIRAH's HVAC&R News explains that this covers active fire and smoke dampers, stair pressurisation and other smoke control, exhaust and air handling systems. Two points from that guidance are worth knowing:

  • Inspection and servicing apply to all fire and smoke control features.
  • Testing requirements apply to systems that operate in fire mode, including those that simply shut down when the alarm activates.

The standard sets out how often each item is checked. A routine fire inspection program should schedule your dampers and smoke control systems alongside the rest of your measures, so nothing is missed.

Records and the logbook

Building Commission NSW says owners must keep maintenance records in the manner prescribed by AS 1851-2012 for at least seven years, and a hard copy logbook must be left on site at the end of testing. Owners must also make these records available for inspection.

Baseline data

AS 1851 relies on "baseline data": the design information that shows how a system is supposed to perform, such as the pressure a stair pressurisation system should achieve. Many older buildings don't have it. According to HVAC&R News, if baseline data is missing, maintenance should continue and the gap should be recorded as a non-conformance, and obtaining the data is the building owner's responsibility. Building Commission NSW says missing baseline data is generally recorded as a non-critical defect or non-conformance.

Common problems with fire dampers

Because dampers are hidden, problems tend to build up quietly over the years. Things that commonly get in the way include:

  • Blocked access panels. A damper needs an access panel so a technician can reach it. New cable trays, pipes, conduits or ceiling grids installed after the damper often block it.
  • Changes to ductwork or ceilings. Renovations and fit-outs can alter or cover dampers without anyone checking they still work.
  • Debris and corrosion that stop the blade or curtain from closing fully.
  • Changes to air-conditioning controls that break the link between the fire alarm and the fire mode of the mechanical system.

If your building is having works done near ductwork or in ceiling spaces, it's worth asking your contractors to keep damper access clear and to tell your fire safety provider about any changes.

What owners and strata managers should do

  1. Check your Fire Safety Schedule to see whether fire dampers, smoke dampers, mechanical air handling or other smoke control measures are listed.
  2. Make sure they're on your maintenance program. Building Commission NSW notes that air-conditioning and mechanical work may need a specialist licence, so confirm who is servicing these systems.
  3. Find out what baseline data you have, and plan to fill any gaps.
  4. Keep access clear and make sure defects raised in reports are dealt with. Building Commission NSW says critical defects must be addressed as soon as possible.
