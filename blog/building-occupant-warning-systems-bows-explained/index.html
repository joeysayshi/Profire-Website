Building Occupant Warning Systems (BOWS) Explained

What a building occupant warning system (BOWS) is, what sets it off, how loud it must be in apartments, and how to keep it working in NSW buildings.

7 November 2026 · Profire Australia

When a fire alarm goes off in an apartment block or office, the sound that tells everyone to get out usually comes from the building occupant warning system, or BOWS. It's one of the less talked-about parts of a fire safety system, but it does the most important job of all: making sure people actually hear the alarm in time to leave. This guide explains what a BOWS is, what sets it off, how loud it needs to be, and what strata managers and building owners should know about keeping it working.

What is a building occupant warning system?

A building occupant warning system is the part of a building's fire alarm setup that sounds a warning through the building so occupants know to evacuate. In most buildings it takes the form of sounders (and sometimes speakers) installed in corridors, common areas and other occupied spaces, connected to the fire detection system.

The requirements come from the National Construction Code (NCC). In NCC 2022, they sit in Specification 20: Smoke detection and alarm systems. Clause S20C7 says a building occupant warning system provided as part of a smoke hazard management system must comply with clause 3.22 of AS 1670.1, the Australian Standard for fire detection and alarm systems, so that it sounds through all occupied areas. The NCC commentary notes this includes outdoor areas people can occupy, like a rooftop terrace.

A BOWS is different from the smoke alarms inside individual apartments. A smoke alarm is a self-contained device that sounds where it is. A BOWS is a building-wide warning that reaches everyone.

What sets off a BOWS?

A BOWS doesn't detect anything by itself. It is triggered by other parts of the fire safety system. Under NCC 2022 Specification 20:

  • Smoke detection systems in Class 2 to 9 buildings must activate a building occupant warning system (S20C4).
  • Smoke alarms in public corridors and internal public spaces of a Class 2 or 3 building (or the Class 4 part of a building) with a smoke alarm system must be connected so they activate a BOWS (S20C3).
  • Smoke detectors that start a smoke control system must also activate a BOWS (S20C5 and S20C6). Detectors that only shut down air-handling systems are an exception.
  • Stair and zone pressurisation systems under AS 1668.1 are controlled by the smoke detection system, and Part E2 of the NCC says activating them also requires activation of a building occupant warning system (E2D3).

In practical terms, if smoke is detected in a common area, the warning should sound across the building, not just near the detector.

How loud does a BOWS need to be in apartments?

The general rule is that the warning must be heard through all occupied areas, measured to AS 1670.1. Apartment buildings get a specific concession, because putting a sounder in every unit isn't always practical.

Under clause S20C7 of NCC 2022, in a Class 2 or 3 building (or the Class 4 part of a building), the sound level doesn't need to be measured inside each sole-occupancy unit (each apartment) if:

  • at least 85 dB(A) is provided at the apartment's entry door, where the building has a smoke alarm system with alarms in the public corridors; or
  • at least 100 dB(A) is provided at the apartment's entry door, where the building has a smoke detection system.

For smoke alarm systems, the NCC allows the built-in sounders of the smoke alarms to be used to meet the requirement in full or in part. The NCC commentary also points out that sounders inside each unit aren't mandatory in residential buildings, because the sound level is measured at the unit entry.

Some fire authorities have published their own interpretations of how these concessions apply. Queensland Fire and Emergency Services, for example, issued a position statement on measuring sound levels for one type of apartment alarm system under the earlier NCC 2019 wording. If you're upgrading or altering a system, your fire engineer, certifier or fire safety practitioner will confirm what applies to your building.

Aged care and health-care buildings

The NCC treats some buildings differently. In Class 3 residential care buildings, Class 9a health-care buildings and Class 9c aged care buildings, the occupant warning must still be arranged, but the alarm's volume and content may be adjusted in resident or ward areas to minimise trauma to the people living or being cared for there. In Class 9c buildings, staff must also be notified.

Is a BOWS part of your annual fire safety statement?

In NSW, the fire safety measures installed in your building are listed on its fire safety schedule, along with the standard each one must perform to. As Lake Macquarie City Council explains, the fire safety schedule, fire safety certificate or annual fire safety statement (AFSS) shows which measures your building has, and those essential fire safety measures must be maintained.

Council lists of statutory fire safety measures include items such as automatic fire detection and alarm systems and emergency warning and intercommunication systems. In many buildings the occupant warning system forms part of the fire detection and alarm system. Exactly how it's described depends on your building's schedule and when the building was approved, so check your fire safety schedule to see how it appears for you.

Whatever the wording, if it's on your schedule, it needs to be maintained and checked as part of your annual fire safety statement.

Common BOWS problems worth checking

You don't need to be a technician to notice when something isn't right. Things that deserve a closer look include:

  • Areas where the alarm is hard to hear, such as rooftops, plant rooms, car parks or new spaces created during renovations.
  • Building changes like new walls, fit-outs or extra doors that block or muffle sound.
  • Damaged, painted-over or removed sounders in corridors and common areas.
  • Alarms that don't sound building-wide when the system is tested.
  • Faults showing on the fire indicator panel that haven't been followed up.

If residents tell you they couldn't hear the alarm during a test or evacuation drill, take it seriously and have it checked.
