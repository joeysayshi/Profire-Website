What to Do When Council Issues a Fire Safety Order

Received a notice of intention or a fire safety order in NSW? Here's what it means, your options, the key deadlines and how to respond step by step.

29 October 2026 · Profire Australia

Few letters make a strata committee or building owner sit up faster than one from council about fire safety. But a notice from council isn't the end of the world, and the way you respond in the first few weeks makes a real difference. This guide explains what to do when council issues a fire safety order in NSW: what the order is, where it comes from, what your options are, and how to get your building back on track.

What is a fire safety order?

Fire safety orders are made under Division 9.3 and Schedule 5 of the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979 (the EP&A Act). They let an authority require a building owner to fix fire safety problems in their building.

Council reports summarising the Act describe the main types of fire safety order:

  • Order No 1 requires a person to do, or stop doing, specified things to ensure or promote adequate fire safety. This is the most common one. It might require upgrades to exits, fire safety equipment or other measures.
  • Order No 2 requires a person to stop an activity on the premises that is, or is likely to be, a life-threatening hazard or a threat to public health or safety.
  • Order No 3 can require people to stop using, evacuate or not enter premises. It can be used where an earlier order hasn't been complied with.

According to Building Commission NSW, both local councils and Fire and Rescue NSW can issue fire safety orders, and an order can require a building to be partly or fully brought up to Building Code of Australia standards.

Why councils issue fire safety orders

There are a few common triggers:

  • A Fire and Rescue NSW inspection. FRNSW has the power to inspect buildings to check whether they have adequate provision for fire safety. When it does, its report and any recommendations go to council. Council must then table the report at a council meeting, decide whether to use its power to give an order, and tell the FRNSW Commissioner what it decided.
  • A council inspection or complaint. Councils run their own fire safety inspection programs and respond to complaints.
  • A missing or late annual fire safety statement. Some councils, such as Wollongong, note that an order can be issued if the owner hasn't submitted the building's annual fire safety statement on time.

One reassuring point from Building Commission NSW: routine inspection and maintenance doesn't by itself trigger an upgrade, and building owners aren't required to upgrade fire safety measures to newer standards. Upgrades become an issue when an existing measure doesn't meet the minimum standard of performance in the building's fire safety schedule, or when an authority orders them.

Step 1: Read the notice of intention carefully

For an Order No 1, council usually can't simply issue the order. It must first give the owner a notice of intention to give the order. Building Commission NSW says the notice must set out the purpose and terms of the proposed order, the time you'd have to comply, and your right to make representations or appeal.

When the notice arrives, check:

  • What council wants done. List every item, and the fire safety measure or part of the building it relates to.
  • The deadline for representations. Wollongong City Council, for example, generally gives owners 21 days from the notice to say whether they want to make representations. Your council's notice will state its own timeframe.
  • The proposed time to comply. This is your starting point for planning works and budget.

If you're a strata manager, get the notice to the committee straight away. Many of the next steps need a decision from the owners corporation, and deadlines don't pause for meeting schedules.

Step 2: Get independent advice

Before you respond, get a clear picture of what's actually wrong and what it would take to fix. That usually means:

  • A fire safety practitioner to inspect the items listed, check them against the building's fire safety schedule and give you a scope of works.
  • A fire safety engineer or certifier where the items involve building design, such as exits, fire separation or compliance with the Building Code of Australia.
  • Legal advice if you're considering challenging the order or the deadlines are unrealistic.

Profire's routine fire inspections come with a photographic report, which can help you show council exactly what condition each measure is in and what has already been fixed.

Step 3: Make representations if you need to

Representations are your chance to respond before the order is finalised. Council must consider them before deciding whether to give the order. You might use them to:

  • Explain works that are already completed or underway.
  • Point out items that are already compliant, with evidence.
  • Ask for more time to comply. Wollongong City Council, for instance, asks that a request for more time includes reasons and a proposed schedule for the works.
  • Propose staged deadlines for larger or more complex works.

Keep representations factual and in writing, and attach supporting evidence such as inspection reports, quotes and certificates.

Step 4: If the order is issued, comply on time

Once an order is issued, it will set out the reasons, the work required and the deadline. Work through it methodically:

  1. Engage qualified contractors for each item, and get written quotes and timeframes.
  2. Keep council updated on progress, especially if anything is likely to slip.
  3. Collect evidence as you go: certificates, test results and photos of completed work.
  4. Submit the documents council asks for by the deadline. Council may inspect the work before confirming you've complied.

Don't let the deadline slide. Wollongong City Council, for example, notes that if an order isn't complied with it can take the owner to court, recover its legal costs, seek substantial fines and issue penalty infringement notices. Councils may also charge compliance cost notices to recover the cost of preparing and monitoring an order.

Can you appeal a fire safety order?

Yes, in many cases. Building Commission NSW says owners can appeal to the Land and Environment Court against an order issued by council. Wollongong City Council notes that owners can appeal all or part of an order within 28 days, so act quickly and get legal advice if you're considering it.

Orders from Fire and Rescue NSW are different. According to Building Commission NSW, you can't appeal a fire safety order issued by FRNSW, except where the order stops someone from using or entering the premises.

After the order: stay on top of compliance

Once council confirms you've complied, the job isn't over. Your building still needs its annual fire safety statement every 12 months, and the fire safety measures need regular maintenance to stay at their required standard. Staying on top of AFSS inspections and routine servicing is the best way to avoid another notice from council.
