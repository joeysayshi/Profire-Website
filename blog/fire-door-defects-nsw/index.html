Fire Doors: Common Defects Found in Inspections

The fire door defects NSW authorities flag, from doors that won't self-close and latch to oversized gaps, wedged-open doors and add-on screen doors.

27 October 2026 · Profire Australia

Fire doors don't look like much. There's no panel, no pump and no flashing light, so they're easy to take for granted. But in a fire, a closed fire door holds back smoke and flames long enough for people to get out and for firefighters to get in. That only works if the door is in good condition and actually shuts. This guide covers the fire door defects that official NSW sources flag, what causes them and what building owners and strata managers can do about them.

Why fire doors matter for compliance

In NSW, fire doors are one of the essential fire safety measures that can be listed on a building's fire safety schedule, alongside things like sprinklers, hydrants, exit signs and emergency lighting. Building owners are responsible for keeping those measures maintained to the standard set out in the schedule, and each year they must have them assessed and issue an annual fire safety statement (AFSS).

The construction requirements for fire doors come from the National Construction Code (NCC), which calls up Australian Standard AS 1905.1 for fire-resistant doorsets. That standard is where most of the rules about closing, latching and gaps come from.

A fire door is only as good as its weakest part. The door leaf, frame, hinges, closer, latch, seals and signage all work together, and a problem with any one of them can stop the door doing its job.

Common fire door defects to look for

1. The door doesn't self-close and latch

This is the defect the Building Commission NSW lists first for fire doors in its Building Defects Library. Under AS 1905.1, a fire doorset must be self-closing, and (other than sliding doors) it must also be self-latching.

In practice, that means if you open the door and let go, it should swing shut on its own and click into the latch without anyone pushing it. Common reasons it doesn't:

  • a closer that has lost tension, leaks oil or has been adjusted too softly
  • hinges that have dropped or loosened, so the door binds on the frame
  • a latch that's been taped, jammed or damaged
  • a door that's warped, or a frame that's moved

A door that closes "most of the way" is still a defect. If it doesn't latch, smoke and fire pressure can push it open.

2. Gaps around the door are too big

Gaps matter because smoke and hot gases travel through them. The Defects Library sets out the clearances AS 1905.1 allows:

  • Door to floor: between 3 mm and 10 mm, including any floor covering (up to 25 mm over a non-combustible threshold).
  • Door to frame (side-hung doors): a mean clearance of no more than 3 mm at the head and sides when closed.
  • Door to doorstop: no more than 3 mm on average, and no more than 5 mm at any point.

Gaps tend to grow over time as hinges wear and doors drop. They're also a common side effect of other work: new flooring that's been laid and then had the door trimmed to suit, or a door that's been planed so it stops sticking.

3. Doors propped open, wedged or blocked

A fire door can only protect people if it's closed when a fire starts. Wedges, door stops, bins, furniture and hooks are all familiar sights in stairwells and corridors, usually because someone wants airflow or an easier trip with the shopping.

It isn't just a maintenance issue. NSW Planning's fire exit notice spells out that it's an offence under the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979 to "interfere with, obstruct or impede the operation of any fire doors", or to put anything in or near a fire exit that could obstruct people moving to and from it. Councils list obstructed fire exit doors among the fire safety offences they enforce.

If a door genuinely needs to be held open during the day, the answer is a compliant hold-open arrangement designed and approved for that door, not a wedge.

4. Security and screen doors added to apartment entry doors

In apartment buildings, the front door of each unit is often a fire door. Some residents add a security or screen door in front of it, which creates problems.

Fire and Rescue NSW (FRNSW) recommends against fitting security or screen doors to apartment entry doors. Its position statement explains why: a screen door makes it more likely the main fire door will be left open for airflow or convenience, and modifying the frame to fit one can compromise the frame's fire rating. FRNSW notes that whether a screen door can be fitted in a particular building is a decision for the regulatory authority (such as Council) or a certifier.

For strata managers, this is worth raising with owners before they install one, not after.

5. Extra bolts and locking hardware

Fire doors need to stay usable for the whole fire, so people can get out and firefighters can get in. FRNSW's position is that fire doors should not be fitted with supplementary bolts or retaining devices that lock the door shut once activated by fire. If those devices trigger too early, they can trap occupants or block the fire brigade. FRNSW also points out that these devices aren't self-latching, and AS 1905.1 doesn't allow hardware that stops a door from self-latching.

The same principle applies to any hardware change. Swapping a lock, closer or hinge for a non-compliant part can quietly turn a compliant fire door into a defective one.

6. Hollow frames

The Defects Library lists unfilled, hollow steel frames as a fire door defect: AS 1905.1 generally requires metal door frames in masonry walls to be backfilled with cement mortar, concrete or non-shrink grout. A hollow frame can be hard to spot without tapping or closer inspection, which is one reason fire doors need a proper check rather than a glance.

How to keep on top of fire door defects

A few simple habits make a big difference between formal inspections:

  1. Do a quick walk-through. Open each fire door, let go, and check it closes and latches on its own.
  2. Look at the gaps. Uneven or large gaps, especially under the door, are a sign that something has moved.
  3. Remove wedges and obstructions whenever you see them, and remind residents and cleaners why.
  4. Check before approving work. New flooring, painting, lock changes and screen doors can all affect a fire door. Ask whether the change will affect its compliance first.
  5. Keep records. Note when defects were found and when they were fixed. That history helps your AFSS practitioner and your committee.
