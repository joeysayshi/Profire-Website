Level 1 vs Level 2 Fire Safety Measures Explained

What Level 1 and Level 2 mean for sprinklers, hydrants and fire alarms in NSW, and how to check your AFSS practitioner is accredited for your building.

23 October 2026 · Profire Australia

If you've been comparing quotes for your annual fire safety statement (AFSS), you may have seen practitioners mention Level 1 and Level 2 fire safety measures. It sounds like a ranking of how important a system is, but it isn't. In NSW, Level 1 and Level 2 describe how far a practitioner's accreditation reaches, and they only apply to a few of the more complex systems in a building.

Here's what the levels mean, which measures they cover, and why they matter when you choose who assesses your building.

Who assesses fire safety measures for an AFSS?

Every year, a building owner with essential fire safety measures must have them assessed and then issue an AFSS. In NSW, assessing those measures and endorsing the statement is a job for an accredited practitioner (fire safety).

The NSW Government recognises one accreditation scheme for this work: the Fire Protection Accreditation Scheme (FPAS), developed by Fire Protection Association Australia (FPA Australia) and approved on 1 July 2020. Accreditation isn't needed to install, maintain, test or service equipment. It's needed for the assessment and endorsement side of the work.

Accreditation under FPAS isn't one blanket licence. It's split by function and, for some measures, by level.

Which fire safety measures have Level 1 and Level 2?

Under FPAS's Fire Safety Assessment accreditation, levels apply to three measures:

  1. Automatic fire suppression systems (sprinklers)
  2. Fire hydrant systems
  3. Automatic fire detection and alarm systems

These are the systems where the design and controls vary the most from one building to the next. The higher level covers more complex installations; the lower level covers a restricted range of simpler ones.

You'll often see these written in lists of fire safety measures as "Automatic fire detection and alarm systems (level 1)" and "(level 2)", which is where the terms come from.

Level 1 vs Level 2 fire safety measures: the differences

The details below come from FPAS Practice Note FSA01, which sets out the accreditation levels and restrictions for fire safety assessment.

Automatic fire detection and alarm systems

  • Level 2 (Advanced): no restriction on scope.
  • Level 1 (Basic): generally limited to systems that don't send multiple programmable outputs to control other systems. It excludes alarm systems that control things like emergency warning and intercommunication systems (EWIS), zoned suppression, zone or air pressurisation, purge systems, smoke exhaust and smoke or heat vents, fans, dampers, louvres, automatic doors, and fire or smoke curtains and shutters.

A Level 1 practitioner can still assess simpler arrangements, including single-zone occupant warning, single-zone door releases and fire alarm monitoring.

In practice, this means a small building with a straightforward fire panel might be within Level 1 scope, while a high-rise where the fire panel triggers pressurisation fans, smoke dampers and an EWIS needs a practitioner accredited at Level 2.

Automatic fire suppression systems (sprinklers)

  • Level 2 (Advanced): any sprinkler system, except combined sprinkler and hydrant systems (more on those below).
  • Level 1 (Basic): a restricted range of systems with less complex hydraulic or hazard considerations. It excludes systems with multiple pressure zones and high hazard systems, such as those protecting high hazard processes or storage, special commodities, or flammable and combustible liquids and other dangerous goods.

Fire hydrant systems

Hydrants follow the same pattern. The higher level covers any hydrant system except combined systems, while the restricted level covers less complex systems and excludes those with multiple pressure zones.

Combined sprinkler and hydrant systems

Some buildings use one combined system for sprinklers and hydrants. To assess it, a practitioner needs accreditation for both sprinklers and hydrants.

What about performance solutions?

A performance solution (once called an alternative solution) doesn't change which level is needed. The practice note says an accredited practitioner can assess a measure's performance whether or not it's subject to a performance solution, as long as the system itself is within their level.

Why the levels matter to building owners

FPAS practitioners may only carry out work within their accredited level. For an owner or strata manager, that has a few practical consequences:

  • Your building's systems decide the level you need. A practitioner with Level 1 detection accreditation can't sign off a complex alarm system that controls smoke management, even if they're accredited for everything else.
  • You may need more than one practitioner. NSW Government guidance for owners notes that because of the range of measures in a building, you may need to select more than one accredited practitioner (fire safety).
  • Don't rely on a title alone. The same guidance recommends checking the register of the relevant accreditation scheme, rather than relying only on a practitioner's own statement, to see exactly what each person is accredited for.
  • If nobody suitable is available, the owner must get authorisation from Building Commission NSW before appointing someone.

How to check you've got the right coverage

A simple approach before your next AFSS:

  1. Get your current fire safety schedule. It lists every measure in your building. If you're not sure where it is, ask your council.
  2. Look for sprinklers, hydrants and detection and alarm systems. These are the measures where levels apply.
  3. Ask how complex they are. Multiple pressure zones, combined sprinkler and hydrant systems, high hazard sprinklers, or an alarm system that controls fans, dampers, pressurisation or an EWIS all point to Level 2.
  4. Check the practitioner's accreditation on the FPAS register and confirm it covers each measure at the level your building needs.
  5. Ask who will endorse what. If more than one practitioner will be involved, find out how the work is split before the inspection is booked.

If you're unsure what's in your building, your current fire safety contractor should be able to explain which systems you have and how they're set up.
